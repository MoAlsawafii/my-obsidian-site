# 1. What Are Learning Disabilities?

## Definition

**Learning disabilities (LDs)** are **neurological disorders** that affect the brain’s ability to:

* receive information,
* process information,
* store information,
* respond to information.

They are **not caused by low intelligence**. Students with LDs are usually of **normal or above-average intelligence**.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
A learning disability is a **neurological difference**, not an intelligence problem.

---

## Important General Facts

* Learning disabilities are more common than many people think.
* They affect approximately **1 in 7 people**.
* The most common learning disability involves difficulty with **language and reading**.
* Many children and adults remain **undiagnosed**, so LDs are often described as a **hidden handicap**.
* If students are undiagnosed, they may experience:

  * poor self-esteem,
  * school failure,
  * difficulty in the workplace,
  * emotional frustration,
  * feeling “not smart” even when they are capable.

---

## Early Identification and Intervention

Early identification is crucial because the earlier a learning disability is detected, the better the child’s chance of success in school and life.

The course material highlights that **67% of young students at risk for reading difficulties achieved average or above-average reading ability when they received early help**.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Early intervention can prevent a learning difficulty from becoming a long-term academic and emotional problem.

---

## What Teachers Should Understand

A teacher’s role is **not to diagnose** the disability. Instead, the teacher should:

* observe warning signs,
* understand how the difficulty affects learning,
* adapt classroom instruction,
* provide support and accommodations,
* focus on strengths,
* avoid blaming the student for something caused by processing difficulty.

---

# 2. The Three Main Areas Where LDs Affect Learning

The slide diagram divides learning difficulties into three broad areas.

## A. Input and Perception

This is about how the student receives or interprets information.

Related conditions:

* **Auditory Processing Disorder (APD)**
* **Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)**

The brain may struggle to:

* receive language accurately,
* hear differences between sounds,
* interpret social or emotional meaning,
* process the raw data of language.

Example:
A student with APD may hear the teacher’s voice but not clearly process the words.

---

## B. Decoding and Processing

This is about how the student understands symbols, sounds, and language structures.

Related conditions:

* **Dyslexia**
* **Specific Language Impairment (SLI)**
* **Down Syndrome**

The brain may struggle to:

* connect letters to sounds,
* decode words,
* understand foundational grammar or syntax,
* process written language.

Example:
A dyslexic student may see the word but struggle to decode it sound by sound.

---

## C. Output and Global Function

This is about how the student produces work, organizes ideas, focuses, or completes tasks.

Related conditions:

* **Dysgraphia**
* **ADHD**
* **Dyscalculia**

The brain may struggle with:

* sustained attention,
* translating thoughts onto paper,
* organizing sequences,
* managing time,
* completing written or mathematical tasks.

Example:
A student with dysgraphia may understand the lesson but cannot express the answer clearly in writing.

---

# 3. The Reading Process

Reading is a **cognitive process**. It is not just looking at words. A reader must understand that:

* spoken language can be written down,
* written language can be read again,
* print represents speech,
* letters and letter patterns represent sounds,
* readers decode print to access meaning,
* the final goal of reading is comprehension.

In English, print is made of:

* alphabet letters,
* letter patterns,
* letter clusters,
* graphemes,
* words,
* sentences.

A beginning reader must learn to associate **letters with sounds** so that they can access the information represented by print.

---

## The Five Critical Areas of Reading Instruction

The course material identifies five essential areas for successful reading instruction.

## 1. Phonemic Awareness

**Phonemic awareness** is the ability to notice, think about, and work with the individual sounds in spoken words.

It means the learner understands that words are made of separate sounds.

Example:
The word *cat* has three sounds: /c/ /a/ /t/.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Phonemic awareness is oral. It deals with sounds in spoken words before reading print.

---

## 2. Phonics

**Phonics** is the relationship between:

* sounds in spoken language, called **phonemes**,
* letters in written language, called **graphemes**.

Phonics helps students remember how to read words by connecting letters and sounds.

Example:
The letter **b** represents the /b/ sound.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Phonics connects sound to print.

---

## 3. Fluency

**Fluency** is the ability to read text:

* accurately,
* quickly,
* smoothly,
* with automatic decoding.

Fluent readers do not spend all their mental energy decoding each word. Because decoding is automatic, they can focus on meaning.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Fluency connects decoding to comprehension.

---

## 4. Vocabulary

**Vocabulary** refers to the words we know and need for communication.

Two important types:

* **Oral vocabulary:** words we use and understand in speech.
* **Reading vocabulary:** words we can recognize and understand in print.

A student may decode a word correctly but still not understand the text if the word is not in their vocabulary.

Example:
A child may sound out the word *enormous*, but if they do not know its meaning, comprehension is still weak.

---

## 5. Text Comprehension

**Comprehension** is the ability to:

* understand meaning from text,
* remember the meaning,
* communicate the meaning,
* monitor whether one understands while reading.

Good readers check themselves while reading.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
The ultimate purpose of reading is **understanding**, not just pronouncing words.

---

# 4. Dyslexia

## Definition

**Dyslexia** is a language-based learning disability involving difficulty with:

* accurate word recognition,
* fluent word recognition,
* spelling,
* decoding,
* processing linguistic and symbolic codes.

It affects the use and processing of:

* alphabetic letters that represent speech sounds,
* symbols,
* sometimes numerical symbols too.

Dyslexia can affect:

* spoken language,
* written language,
* language comprehension.

It is the **most common learning disability**, affecting nearly **10% of the population**.

Important characteristics:

* It is not caused by lack of intelligence.
* It is not caused by lack of motivation.
* It is not caused by poor education.
* It is linked to neurological development.
* It often runs in families.
* It can be mild, moderate, or severe.
* Children do not simply “grow out of it.”

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Dyslexia is not laziness and not low intelligence. It is a difficulty in processing language symbols and sounds.

---

## Dyslexia Symptoms by Age

## First Grade

A first-grade student may:

* struggle to link letters with sounds,
* fail to connect **b** with /b/,
* have trouble matching sounds to letters at the beginning, middle, or end of short words,
* struggle with common blends and digraphs,
* struggle with patterns such as silent-e words or adjacent vowels.

Examples:

* blends: **bl**, **st**, **tr**
* digraphs: **sh**, **ch**, **th**

⭐ **Exam focus:**
In early grades, dyslexia appears as difficulty connecting letters and sounds.

---

## Second Grade

By second grade, the basic tools for reading should usually be in place.

Warning signs include:

* not sounding out words,
* taking wild guesses,
* being unable to read unfamiliar grade-level words,
* failing to decode most single-syllable words,
* struggling with easy multisyllabic words,
* not noticing the internal parts of words,
* not building a bank of fluently recognized words,
* not enjoying reading.

Examples of expected multisyllabic words:

* *rabbit*
* *butter*
* *sleepy*

A dyslexic student may read by guessing instead of decoding.

---

## Third Grade and Above

Reading demands increase after third grade because students are expected to use reading to learn new information.

Warning signs include:

* slow, choppy oral reading,
* word omissions,
* word substitutions,
* mispronunciations,
* poor spelling,
* avoidance of reading aloud,
* difficulty reading fluently,
* trouble learning a foreign language,
* homework taking very long,
* exhaustion from reading,
* emotional pain and low self-esteem.

A major sign is **context guessing**. The student may substitute a word that makes sense in the sentence but does not match the printed word.

Example:
The student reads **car** instead of **automobile**.

The meaning may be similar, but the student did not decode the actual printed word.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Repeated meaningful substitutions show that the student may be relying on context instead of decoding.

---

## Dyslexia’s Effects on Reading

A dyslexic learner may show:

* very slow progress in reading,
* no clear strategy for reading new words,
* difficulty reading unfamiliar words,
* wild guessing instead of systematic sounding out,
* difficulty reading small function words such as *that*, *an*, *in*,
* trouble with multisyllabic words,
* omission of word parts,
* failure to decode the middle of words,
* fear of reading aloud,
* avoidance of oral reading,
* substitutions,
* omissions,
* mispronunciations,
* choppy and labored reading,
* reading without expression,
* reading that sounds like a foreign language,
* reliance on context to guess meaning,
* better understanding of words in context than isolated words,
* poor performance on multiple-choice tests,
* inability to finish tests on time,
* replacing difficult words with similar-meaning words,
* disastrous spelling,
* spellings that spell-check may not catch,
* difficulty reading math word problems,
* very slow and tiring reading,
* homework that seems endless,
* parents often having to read to the child,
* messy handwriting despite good typing skills,
* extreme difficulty learning foreign languages,
* lack of enjoyment in reading,
* avoidance of reading for pleasure,
* improved accuracy over time but continued lack of fluency,
* low self-esteem,
* hidden emotional pain,
* family history of reading, spelling, or foreign-language difficulties.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Dyslexia affects decoding, fluency, spelling, reading confidence, test timing, and emotional wellbeing.

---

## Dyslexia and Self-Esteem

Dyslexia can cause emotional pain because the student may:

* believe they are “dumb,”
* avoid school,
* become moody,
* feel embarrassed,
* lose confidence,
* become afraid of reading aloud.

The slides highlight that teachers may see slow reading and poor spelling, but the student may carry frustration and low self-esteem.

---

## Dyslexia: Effective Teaching Solutions

The strongest teaching approach is:

* systematic,
* explicit,
* sequential,
* cumulative phonics instruction.

Students should not be expected to “pick it up” through exposure alone.

They need to be directly taught the code of language.

---

## Orton-Gillingham Principles

Orton-Gillingham instruction is based on the idea that struggling readers benefit from structured patterns of explicit and systematic phonics.

Main principles:

## 1. Multisensory Applications

Instruction should engage:

* visual cues,
* auditory cues,
* kinesthetic movement.

Example:
A student may see the letter, say the sound, and trace the letter.

---

## 2. Sequential Alphabet and Phonics Instruction

Skills should be taught in a clear order.

Students should first learn:

* simple one-to-one letter-sound relationships,
* then vowel sounds,
* then long and short vowel patterns,
* then letter groups,
* then rules for pronunciation patterns.

---

## 3. Practical Language-Based Applications

Students need to apply phonics to real reading tasks.

This means teaching should not stay only at the level of isolated drills. Students must connect skills to actual reading.

---

## 4. Logical Repetition

Repetition helps learners recognize patterns and make predictions about new words.

The goal is for students to see familiar patterns in unfamiliar words.

---

## Important Phonics Content Mentioned

Students need to understand:

* 42 sounds,
* letter groups,
* diphthongs,
* digraphs,
* “pig pens,”
* five phonetic skills,
* two decoding skills,
* the idea that two letters can represent one speech sound.

Example:
**sh** has two letters but one sound.

---

## Teacher Implications for Dyslexia

Teachers should:

* provide audiobooks so students can access grade-level content,
* allow extra time for tests,
* allow extra time for reading assignments,
* focus on content rather than spelling in early drafts,
* avoid assuming the student is careless,
* provide explicit phonics,
* support fluency development,
* reduce embarrassment around oral reading,
* provide a quiet space for reading or tests when possible.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
The teacher should separate **intellectual ability** from **reading output**. A dyslexic student may understand complex ideas but struggle to access printed words.

# 5. Autism Spectrum Disorder

## Definition

**Autism** is a complex developmental disability that affects:

* social interaction,
* communication,
* behavior,
* leisure/play activities.

It usually appears during the first two years of life and results from a neurological disorder affecting brain functioning.

It is a **spectrum disorder**, meaning symptoms and severity vary greatly between individuals.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Autism is a spectrum. Do not assume all autistic learners have the same abilities or challenges.

---

## Core Symptom Areas

## 1. Social Interaction and Relationships

Students may have difficulty with:

* eye contact,
* facial expressions,
* body posture,
* nonverbal communication,
* developing friendships,
* sharing interests or achievements,
* empathy,
* understanding others’ feelings.

This does not mean the student has no feelings. It means they may struggle to interpret or express social meaning.

---

## 2. Verbal and Nonverbal Communication

Students may show:

* delayed speech,
* lack of speech,
* difficulty starting conversations,
* difficulty continuing conversations,
* echolalia,
* literal interpretation of language,
* difficulty understanding jokes,
* difficulty understanding sarcasm,
* difficulty understanding implied meanings.

**Echolalia** means repeating phrases heard before.

Example:
A child repeats a line from a TV show or repeats the teacher’s phrase.

The material notes that as many as **40% of people with autism may never speak**.

---

## 3. Limited Interests, Activities, or Play

Students may show:

* unusual focus on parts of objects,
* focus on wheels of a toy car rather than the whole car,
* deep interest in narrow topics,
* fascination with video games, trading cards, license plates, trains, weather, etc.,
* need for sameness,
* strong routines,
* stereotyped behaviors.

Examples of stereotyped behaviors:

* body rocking,
* hand flapping.

---

## Symptoms During Childhood

Parents or caregivers usually notice symptoms during the first three years.

Possible signs:

* child does not like being held,
* child does not enjoy games like peek-a-boo,
* child does not begin talking,
* child begins talking and then loses language skills,
* child seems not to hear sometimes,
* child reacts to distant background sounds at other times.

Important implication:
The child’s hearing may appear inconsistent, but the issue may be related to attention, perception, or processing rather than ordinary hearing loss.

---

## Symptoms During Teen Years

Teenagers with autism may gain skills but still struggle with:

* understanding others,
* relationships,
* social communication,
* puberty,
* sexuality,
* depression,
* anxiety,
* epilepsy.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Needs may change with age. Autism does not disappear in adolescence.

---

## Symptoms in Adulthood

Some adults with autism:

* work,
* live independently,
* succeed professionally,
* have average or above-average intelligence.

Others need:

* part-time supervision,
* full-time supervision,
* residential support.

The degree of independence is related to:

* intelligence,
* communication ability,
* support needs.

The material notes that at least **33%** achieve at least partial independence.

---

## Other Autism-Related Symptoms

Some people with autism may have:

* ADHD-like symptoms,
* unusual sensory perceptions,
* strong food likes/dislikes,
* unusual preoccupations,
* sleep problems.

The material notes:

* about **10%** may have savant skills,
* sleep problems occur in about **40–70%**.

Examples of sensory differences:

* light touch may feel painful,
* deep pressure may feel calming,
* some may not feel pain normally.

---

## Other Conditions Related to Autism

Autism is part of a broader category historically called:

* Pervasive Developmental Disorders,
* Autism Spectrum Disorders.

It can overlap or be confused with:

* Asperger’s syndrome,
* PDD-NOS.

PDD-NOS refers to cases where children show similar behaviors but do not meet the full criteria for autism.

---

# 6. How Autism Affects Reading and Language Learning

Autism affects reading mainly through communication, language processing, and social understanding.

Students may:

* have delayed or absent verbal language,
* need alternative communication systems,
* develop literacy slowly,
* not develop literacy without support,
* have difficulty connecting spoken language to written language,
* be strong visual learners,
* interpret text literally,
* struggle with inference,
* struggle with abstract thinking,
* misunderstand metaphors,
* misunderstand character motivation,
* struggle with fiction and poetry,
* find group reading discussions difficult,
* struggle with literature requiring perspective-taking.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Autistic students may decode words but struggle with inference, figurative language, perspective-taking, and abstract comprehension.

---

## Why Reading May Receive Less Attention

Because autistic children may have many social, behavioral, and communication needs, literacy can sometimes be given lower priority.

The material points out that special education training may focus more on:

* behavior modification,
* behavior management,

than on teaching reading and writing.

Teacher implication:
Literacy should still be taught. Autism does not mean reading should be ignored.

---

# 7. Reading Solutions for Autism

## Main Principles

Autistic learners often benefit from instruction that is:

* visual,
* simple,
* explicit,
* concise,
* structured,
* predictable,
* connected to the child’s interests,
* connected to personal experience,
* phonics-based when possible.

---

## Three Essential Components

The ebook identifies three important components:

## 1. Visual Presentation

Many autistic learners are visual learners.

Use:

* pictures,
* graphic organizers,
* schedules,
* picture cards,
* written words,
* visual models.

---

## 2. Simple and Minimal Directions

Instructions should be:

* short,
* direct,
* concrete,
* broken into small steps.

Avoid long verbal explanations.

---

## 3. Visual and Simple Phonics Instruction

Many students benefit from phonics instruction when it is presented:

* visually,
* clearly,
* simply,
* systematically.

This connects to the idea that some autistic learners have language-processing difficulties similar to dyslexia.

---

## Classroom Strategies for Autism

Teachers should:

* use visual supports extensively,
* use graphic organizers,
* use schedules,
* use picture cards,
* provide explicit instructions,
* break tasks into small manageable steps,
* choose reading texts based on student interests,
* begin with reality-based texts before fantasy,
* pre-teach abstract concepts,
* pre-teach social situations in stories,
* create predictable classroom routines,
* reduce anxiety through structure,
* make reading enjoyable,
* use the student’s special interests to increase attention.

Examples:

* If the student likes trains, use train-related vocabulary or stories.
* If the student struggles with fantasy, begin with stories about daily routines before stories with talking animals or magical characters.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
For autism, “visual + explicit + interest-based + predictable” is a key teaching formula.

# 8. ADHD

## Definition

**ADHD** stands for **Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder**.

It is a **neurobehavioral developmental disorder** characterized by persistent patterns of:

* inattention,
* hyperactivity,
* impulsivity.

It can affect how children:

* act,
* think,
* feel,
* focus,
* perform in school.

ADHD affects about **3–5% of school-age children** and is estimated to be **three or four times more common in boys**.

Some children improve around puberty, but for others ADHD continues into adulthood if untreated.

---

## Important Clarification

ADHD is **not classified as a learning disability** in the same way dyslexia is.

A learning disability usually affects one or two specific academic areas while other areas may be average or strong.

ADHD affects learning more globally.

The material uses the analogy:

* A specific LD is like one or two lights being turned off in a house.
* ADHD is like all the lights being dimmed.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
ADHD affects many cognitive functions globally, especially focus and executive function.

---

## ADHD Subtypes

## 1. Inattentive Type

Signs include:

* difficulty paying attention to details,
* careless errors,
* difficulty sustaining attention,
* apparent listening problems,
* difficulty following instructions,
* organization problems,
* avoiding tasks requiring mental effort,
* losing things,
* distractibility,
* forgetfulness.

---

## 2. Hyperactive-Impulsive Type

Signs include:

* fidgeting,
* squirming,
* difficulty staying seated,
* excessive running or climbing,
* difficulty playing quietly,
* always seeming “on the go,”
* excessive talking,
* blurting answers before hearing the full question,
* difficulty waiting,
* interrupting,
* intruding.

---

## 3. Combined Type

The combined type includes both:

* inattentive symptoms,
* hyperactive-impulsive symptoms.

It is the most common subtype.

---

# 9. How ADHD Affects Reading

ADHD affects reading because reading often requires:

* sustained attention,
* working memory,
* self-monitoring,
* quiet focus,
* organization,
* comprehension strategies.

Students with ADHD may struggle with:

* long reading passages,
* noisy classrooms,
* distracting environments,
* summarization,
* remembering plot details,
* following multi-step instructions,
* holding key information in working memory,
* checking comprehension while reading.

Example:
A student may read a page but not remember what happened because attention shifted during reading.

---

## ADHD and Executive Function

Executive function includes skills such as:

* planning,
* organizing,
* remembering instructions,
* monitoring attention,
* controlling impulses,
* completing tasks.

In reading, executive function is needed for:

* summarizing,
* identifying main ideas,
* remembering details,
* connecting ideas,
* following a sequence.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
ADHD may not prevent decoding, but it can strongly affect comprehension because comprehension requires attention and working memory.

---

## Reading Solutions for ADHD

Teachers should:

* provide short reading segments,
* use 10-minute reading increments,
* give breaks,
* choose books that match student interests,
* provide quiet reading areas,
* reduce distractions,
* teach comprehension strategies explicitly,
* break strategies into manageable steps,
* use bookmarks or guide tools to track lines,
* teach note-taking,
* teach summarization step by step,
* check understanding often,
* allow distraction-free testing spaces.

The slides call this:

* **time chunking**,
* **tracking tools**,
* **active strategies**,
* **quiet environment**.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
For ADHD, instruction should reduce cognitive overload and support attention.

# 10. Auditory Processing Disorder

## Definition

**Auditory Processing Disorder (APD)** is a difficulty in how the brain recognizes, distinguishes, and interprets sounds.

It is not the same as deafness or being hard of hearing.

In APD:

* the ears may work normally,
* hearing may be normal,
* intelligence may be normal,
* the brain struggles to process the sounds.

The slide describes it as a “scrambled frequency”: the ears function normally, but the brain struggles to interpret the sounds they send.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
APD is a processing problem, not a hearing problem.

---

## Symptoms of APD

Students may:

* have trouble paying attention to orally presented information,
* have trouble remembering oral information,
* struggle with multi-step oral instructions,
* have poor listening skills,
* need more time to process information,
* show low academic performance,
* show behavioral problems,
* have language difficulty,
* confuse syllable sequences,
* struggle with vocabulary development,
* struggle with understanding spoken language,
* have difficulty with reading,
* have difficulty with comprehension,
* have difficulty with spelling,
* have difficulty with vocabulary,
* frequently ask “What?”,
* need information repeated,
* be easily distracted by background noise.

---

## What the Teacher Sees vs What the Student Feels

## Teacher sees:

* does not follow instructions,
* asks for repetition,
* appears distracted,
* struggles in discussions.

## Student feels:

* overwhelmed by too much verbal information,
* frustrated because others understand easily,
* embarrassed to ask for repetition.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
A student with APD may look inattentive, but the real problem may be auditory processing.

---

## APD Reflection Table

| Observation              | Wrong Assumption     | Reality               |
| ------------------------ | -------------------- | --------------------- |
| Misses instructions      | Not paying attention | Processing difficulty |
| Asks for repetition      | Careless             | Needs time to process |
| Struggles in discussions | Low ability          | Auditory challenge    |

---

# 11. Common Areas Affected by APD

APD can affect several auditory-language areas.

## 1. Phonological Awareness

This is understanding that language is made of individual sounds called phonemes.

A child with weak phonological awareness may struggle to:

* recognize individual sounds in words,
* isolate sounds,
* identify rhyming words,
* count the number of sounds in a word,
* understand spoken language,
* read,
* write.

This is a fundamental precursor to reading.

---

## 2. Auditory Discrimination

This is the ability to hear differences between sounds.

Students may struggle to distinguish:

* coat / boat,
* /ch/ /sh/,
* 17 / 70.

This affects reading and spelling because students may not attach the correct sound to the correct letter.

---

## 3. Auditory Memory

This is the ability to store and recall verbally presented information.

Students may struggle to:

* follow spoken instructions,
* remember details from a story read aloud,
* recall lecture information.

---

## 4. Auditory Sequencing

This is the ability to remember or reconstruct the order of sounds or items.

Example:
Saying or writing **ephelant** instead of **elephant**.

This affects spelling, reading, and following ordered instructions.

---

## 5. Auditory Blending

This is putting individual sounds together to form words.

Example:
/c/ + /a/ + /t/ becomes **cat**.

If auditory blending is weak, decoding becomes more difficult.

---

# 12. How APD Affects Reading

Not every reading disorder comes from APD, and not every child with APD will have reading problems. However, reading has an auditory component.

APD affects reading because students may:

* not hear differences between similar sounds,
* struggle with phonological awareness,
* struggle to connect phonemes to graphemes,
* decode less accurately,
* spell poorly,
* mishear words,
* develop weak vocabulary,
* misunderstand stories,
* become distracted by background noise,
* use excessive mental energy on decoding,
* have less mental energy left for comprehension.

Example:
If a student cannot hear the difference between **coat** and **boat**, connecting sounds to letters becomes difficult.

---

## APD and Vocabulary

Children with APD often have low vocabulary because during early language development they may:

* miss sounds,
* mix sounds,
* mishear words,
* misunderstand word meanings,
* fail to build strong sound-word connections.

Teacher implication:

* pre-teach unfamiliar vocabulary,
* use written words,
* use pictures,
* check pronunciation,
* monitor understanding.

---

# 13. Teaching Strategies for APD

## General Classroom Strategies

Teachers should:

* reduce background noise,
* seat the student near the teacher,
* seat the student away from noise sources such as doors or heaters,
* use visual aids,
* pair oral instructions with written instructions,
* speak slightly slower,
* use a mildly increased volume,
* pause frequently,
* use simple expressive sentences,
* ask the child to repeat directions back,
* give one task at a time,
* provide written notes,
* provide routines and schedules,
* teach the child to move to quieter places when listening is necessary,
* use explicit multisensory phonics instruction.

---

## Home/School Strategies

Helpful strategies include:

* quiet study place,
* peaceful organized lifestyle,
* good eating habits,
* good sleeping habits,
* regular realistic chores,
* neat room and desk,
* self-esteem building,
* written reminders,
* watches,
* routines,
* scheduling.

---

## Five Things Teachers Need to Know About APD

The material presents teacher-facing guidance:

1. The child has trouble listening because of a learning problem, not a behavior problem.
2. The child needs to hear things more than once.
3. Important materials should be sent home for review.
4. The child may struggle to filter background noise.
5. The child benefits from clear sight of the teacher and visual cues.
6. Home and school communication should remain open.

---

## Typical Teacher vs Special Guide

## Typical untrained response:

* repeats instructions louder,
* assumes the student is distracted,
* continues without checking understanding,
* gives verbal instructions only.

## Trained support response:

* simplifies instructions,
* chunks instructions,
* uses written and visual supports,
* checks understanding step by step,
* reduces background noise.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Repeating louder does not solve APD. The issue is processing, not volume.

# 15. Dysgraphia

## Definition

**Dysgraphia** is a learning disability that affects writing ability.

It can affect:

* handwriting,
* spelling,
* punctuation,
* capitalization,
* grammar,
* putting thoughts on paper,
* organizing written output.

It is often connected with dyslexia because both involve language processing, sequencing, and directionality.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Dysgraphia is not just “messy handwriting.” It affects the whole process of producing written language.

* slow writing,

* unusual starting and ending points,

* capitalization problems,
* punctuation problems,
* poor spatial organization,
* words spaced too widely,
* words pushed too close together,
* directionality issues,
* sequencing difficulties,
* difficulty organizing ideas for essays,
* avoidance of writing tasks.

# 17. Strategies for Dysgraphia

The ebook provides many strategies. They can be grouped for easier revision, but all key strategies are preserved.

## A. Planning and Organizing Ideas

Teachers should:

* encourage students to outline thoughts,
* help students get main ideas down before worrying about spelling or punctuation,
* have students draw a picture of each paragraph’s idea,
* use visual graphic organizers,
* use mind maps,
* write one key word or phrase for each paragraph, then add details later,
* separate writing into stages:

  * brainstorming,
  * drafting,
  * editing,
  * proofreading,
  * revising.

---

## B. Dictation and Oral Support

Teachers can:

* allow students to dictate ideas into a recorder,
* allow students to listen and write later,
* let students dictate assignments or tests,
* allow a scribe,
* train the scribe to write exactly what the student says,
* allow the student to revise the dictated text afterward,
* allow oral tests,
* allow taped assignments,
* allow oral reports.

---

## C. Technology Support

Students should be allowed to:

* practice keyboarding,
* use a computer,
* use spellcheck,
* use speaking spellcheckers,
* use speech-recognition software if appropriate,
* type rough drafts,
* copy and revise drafts digitally.

Important note:
Keyboarding may be difficult at first, but after learning the key patterns, typing can become faster and clearer than handwriting.

---

## D. Handwriting Practice and Fine Motor Support

Students should continue practicing handwriting because handwriting may still be needed in life.

Teachers can:

* build handwriting instruction into the schedule,
* use multisensory handwriting techniques,
* practice letters until they become automatic,
* teach alternative handwriting methods such as **Handwriting Without Tears**,
* allow cursive or manuscript, whichever is more legible,
* allow different paper types,
* allow raised-line paper for younger students,
* allow older students to choose line width,
* allow different writing instruments,
* allow different colors of paper or writing tools.

---

## E. Tactile and Fine Motor Memory

The slides include “Pillar 1: Tactile & Fine Motor Memory.”

## Feel the Letter

* Trace letters in sand, salt, or the air.
* Benefit: builds letter memory and confidence before using a pencil.

## Start Big, Finish Small

* Write huge letters on a whiteboard.
* Then write medium letters on paper.
* Then write small letters in a notebook.
* Benefit: gradually reduces hand movement and mechanical pressure.

## Strong Fingers

* Squeeze playdough.
* Use tweezers.
* Draw shapes.
* Cut paper carefully.
* Benefit: strengthens grip and fine motor control before formal writing tasks.

---

## F. Extra Time and Reduced Copying

Teachers should:

* allow more time for written tasks,
* allow more time for note-taking,
* allow more time for copying,
* allow more time on tests,
* give assignments early,
* allow students to begin projects early,
* reduce copying demands,
* provide worksheets with problems already written,
* provide partial outlines for notes,
* provide photocopied notes,
* provide major headings and let students fill in details,
* provide details and let students add headings.

---

## G. Grading Accommodations

Teachers should:

* remove neatness as a grading criterion for some assignments,
* remove spelling as a grading criterion for some assignments,
* focus on selected parts of writing,
* grade the writing process in stages,
* give points for brainstorming,
* give points for rough drafts,
* give points for final drafts,
* focus on content first and mechanics second.

---

## H. Note-Taking and Abbreviation Support

Teachers can:

* allow abbreviations such as **b/c** for *because*,
* help the student develop an abbreviation notebook,
* use a note-buddy,
* conduct note checks,
* provide outlines,
* reduce frantic copying.

---

## I. Long-Term Assignment Support

Teachers should:

* provide extra structure,
* use intermittent deadlines,
* check progress periodically,
* communicate with parents,
* allow after-school work with the teacher if deadlines are missed.

---

## J. Cooperative Writing Projects

Students can work in roles such as:

* brainstormer,
* organizer of information,
* writer,
* proofreader,
* illustrator.

This allows the dysgraphic student to contribute without every task depending on handwriting.

---

## K. Fatigue Strategies

If students become fatigued, they can:

* shake hands quickly but not violently,
* rub hands together and notice warmth,
* rub hands on carpet in circles,
* rub hands on textured clothing near the knees,
* click a pen with thumb or index finger,
* do sitting pushups by pressing palms on the chair and lifting the body slightly.

---

## L. POWER Writing Sequence

The material suggests remembering writing stages through **POWER**:

* **P** = Plan your paper
* **O** = Organize your thoughts and ideas
* **W** = Write your draft
* **E** = Edit your work
* **R** = Revise your work and produce a final draft

⭐ **Exam focus:**
POWER is likely testable because it is a memorable classroom strategy.

---

## M. Supportive Classroom Ecosystem

## Do:

* praise effort, not only perfect handwriting,
* allow extra time,
* permit oral answers,
* use worksheets with enough physical space,
* provide clear examples before requiring writing.

## Do not:

* demand perfect handwriting at the expense of ideas,
* embarrass the student in front of the class,
* compare the student to peers.

# 18. Dyscalculia

## Definition

**Dyscalculia** is a broad term for severe difficulties in mathematics.

It can include difficulties ranging from:

* inability to understand number meaning,
* inability to apply math principles,
* difficulty solving math problems,
* difficulty with math facts,
* difficulty with symbols,
* difficulty with money,
* difficulty with time.

Just as dyslexia refers to reading difficulties, dyscalculia refers to mathematical difficulties in individuals of average intelligence.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
Dyscalculia is not simply being “bad at math.” It is a learning disability affecting number sense and mathematical processing.

---

## Causes or Underlying Weaknesses

Dyscalculia can be linked to weaknesses in:

## 1. Memory

The student may struggle to remember:

* arithmetic facts,
* rules,
* formulas,
* problem-solving steps,
* meanings of symbols such as + or −.

---

## 2. Reasoning and Logical Thinking

Weak reasoning may cause difficulty:

* choosing the correct operation,
* differentiating addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division,
* selecting alternative strategies,
* transferring skills to new problems.

---

## 3. Visual-Spatial Relationships

Weak visual-spatial skills may cause:

* misaligned numbers in columns,
* difficulty understanding fractions,
* confusion with decimal place value,
* difficulty sorting objects by size, shape, or color,
* difficulty understanding written information on the board or in textbooks.

---

## 4. Math Anxiety

A student may also have math difficulty because of intense anxiety.

Math anxiety involves:

* overwhelming lack of confidence,
* fear of math,
* emotional shutdown,
* avoidance of math tasks.

---

# 23. Specific Language Impairment

## Definition

**Specific Language Impairment (SLI)** is a significant delay in oral language development.

It appears in the preschool years before formal schooling.

The delay is not due to:

* hearing loss,
* low intelligence,
* other medical conditions.

SLI can be diagnosed by a speech-language pathologist.

It affects about **5–10% of preschoolers** and may have a genetic basis.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
SLI is about oral language development, not low intelligence or hearing loss.

---

## Components of Language Affected by SLI

SLI can affect:

## 1. Grammar and Syntax

Students may struggle with:

* verb tense,
* word order,
* sentence structure,
* complex sentences.

Example:
“He walk” instead of “He walks.”

---

## 2. Semantics

Semantics refers to meaning and vocabulary.

Students may have:

* weak vocabulary,
* word-finding difficulties,
* difficulty learning new words.

Example:
The child may often use vague words like “thing” or “stuff.”

---

## 3. Phonology

Phonology involves sound awareness.

Students may struggle with:

* rhyming,
* identifying sounds in words,
* phonological awareness.

---

## 4. Receptive and Expressive Language

SLI may involve:

* **receptive difficulties:** problems understanding language,
* **expressive difficulties:** problems using language.

Important distinction:
A child whose only issue is stuttering does not have SLI, because SLI is not mainly about the motor act of producing words.

---

## Symptoms of SLI

Students may show:

* short sentences,
* simple sentences,
* difficulty producing complex sentences,
* difficulty understanding complex sentences,
* poor vocabulary,
* word-finding problems,
* difficulty learning new words,
* grammar errors,
* verb tense errors,
* word order errors,
* weak phonological awareness.

---

## What the Teacher Sees vs What the Student Feels

## Teacher sees:

* limited vocabulary,
* short or incomplete sentences,
* difficulty expressing ideas.

## Student feels:

* frustrated when unable to express thoughts,
* low confidence in speaking,
* avoidance of participation.

---

## Teacher vs Special Guide

## Typical untrained response:

* corrects mistakes quickly without explanation,
* assumes the student is weak in English,
* calls on more confident students,
* focuses on fluency over development.

## Trained support response:

* models correct sentence structures,
* uses scaffolding,
* provides sentence starters,
* uses prompts,
* gives extra processing time,
* gives extra response time,
* encourages safe participation.

---

# 24. SLI and Reading Disabilities

SLI puts children at clear risk for later academic difficulties, especially reading disabilities.

The material states that **40–75% of children with SLI may have problems learning to read**.

Reading depends on many of the same skills affected by SLI:

* grammar,
* syntax,
* vocabulary,
* phonological awareness,
* language comprehension.

Even children who appear to have “caught up” by kindergarten remain at higher risk than children with no history of SLI.

The highest risk is for children whose language difficulties are:

* persistent over time,
* severe,
* affecting multiple language components.

⭐ **Exam focus:**
A history of SLI matters even if the child no longer qualifies for speech-language services.

---

## Reading Solutions for SLI

Parents and teachers should seek evaluation from a qualified speech-language specialist.

Possible supports:

* developmental screening through school districts,
* university speech-language clinics,
* birth-to-three services,
* preschool services,
* speech-language therapy,
* parent involvement,
* home language activities.

Early intervention can help:

* language growth,
* social growth,
* school readiness,
* communication,
* reduction of behavior problems caused by frustration.

---

## School-Based Support for SLI

Schools should:

* use a comprehensive reading curriculum,
* provide explicit systematic instruction,
* teach phonemic awareness,
* teach phonics,
* teach fluency,
* teach vocabulary,
* teach comprehension,
* share SLI history during transition to kindergarten,
* monitor children closely,
* watch for early signs of reading difficulty,
* monitor phonemic awareness,
* monitor letter-sound knowledge,
* provide prompt intervention,
* integrate speech-language services with reading instruction.

* Define SLI and explain its link to reading disability.
* Explain the teacher’s role: observe, understand, adapt, not diagnose.

---

# 28. Final Course Takeaway

Learning disabilities are common neurological differences. They do not mean the student is unintelligent or incapable. The central teacher responsibility is to understand the barrier, avoid harmful assumptions, and provide structured, explicit, multisensory, patient, and strength-based support. When students receive the right tools, they can access learning that would otherwise remain blocked.
