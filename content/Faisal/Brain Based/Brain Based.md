## 1. Main Idea of Chapter 11: What Is Brain-Based Teaching?

Chapter 11 answers one central question:

**How can teachers put brain research into actual classroom practice before, during, and after teaching?**

Jensen explains that a “brain-based” classroom is not based on one perfect method. There is **no single best way to teach**. Many children were educated successfully before modern brain research existed. However, teachers today can improve their teaching by aligning instruction with what is known about how the brain and mind learn. 

**Key idea:** Brain-based teaching is not a fixed teaching recipe. It is a model that helps teachers plan learning in a way that supports the brain’s natural learning processes.

**Exam focus:**
A teacher may ask: *Does Jensen believe there is one perfect method for brain-based teaching?*
Answer: **No.** He says there is no “perfect” or “best” way to teach. The model is a guide for thinking and planning, not a rigid formula.

---

## 2. Jensen’s Teaching Model: The 10–80–10 Structure

Jensen organizes brain-based teaching into a **three-stage model**:

1. **Before class / before learning — 10%**
2. **During class / during learning — 80%**
3. **After class / after learning — 10%**

The book’s Figure 11.1 presents this as a teaching model, while the slide deck calls it the **“10-80-10 Temporal Lesson Architecture.”** The idea is that learning does not only happen while the teacher is presenting content. It begins before the lesson and continues after the lesson.  

### The model includes these processes:

### Before learning — 10%

* **Prepare** the learners.
* **Create** an optimal learning environment.

### During learning — 80%

* **Engage** learners emotionally and physically.
* **Frame** the learning so it feels relevant, important, and compelling.
* **Acquire** knowledge, skills, values, and experiences.
* **Elaborate** through trial and error, feedback, and active processing.
* **Connect** learning to other content, processes, and the self.

### After learning — 10%

* **Settle** the learning through passive processing.
* **Rehearse and incorporate** by revising and using the learning.

**Exam focus:**
Memorize the order:

**Prepare → Create → Engage → Frame → Acquire → Elaborate → Connect → Settle → Rehearse and Incorporate**

---

## 3. Important Warning: Do Not Misuse the Model

Jensen warns that people may try to compare this model with older educational models, such as those of:

* John Dewey
* David Kolb
* Madeline Hunter
* Bernice McCarthy’s 4-MAT system

He says teachers should not force this model to fit older theories. Each theory was designed for specific reasons and should be respected on its own terms. Jensen’s model is different because it is based specifically on research about the **brain and mind**, not only philosophy or general pedagogy. 

**In-between-the-lines implication:**
The teacher should not say, “This is just the same as another teaching model.” Jensen wants the reader to understand this as a brain-and-mind-based model with its own purpose.

**Exam focus:**
The model is:

* **Sequential**
* Divided into **three stages**
* Based on **brain and mind research**
* A guide for lesson design, not a strict universal formula

# Stage 1: Before Class / Before Learning — 10%

The first stage focuses on what the teacher does **before class begins**. Jensen says this stage increases the chances of instructional success. It includes two major parts:

1. **Pre-exposure**
2. **The physical learning environment** 

The slide deck describes this stage as **“Dual Preparation”** because the teacher prepares both:

* The **space** externally
* The **mind** internally 

---

## 4. Pre-Exposure

Pre-exposure means preparing students’ brains before the lesson officially begins. Some suggestions help the teacher, while others help both the teacher and the students.

Jensen says teachers should prepare for each class **long in advance**. This preparation should be:

* Mental
* Academic
* Emotional 

### Teacher preparation includes:

### 1. Thinking about particular students

The teacher should identify students who need extra support. This includes students who are:

* Behind the rest of the class
* Ahead of the rest of the class

The teacher should plan ahead to help both groups succeed.

**Implication:**
Brain-based teaching is not only about the average student. It requires attention to individual differences.

### 2. Walking through the lesson in advance

The teacher should mentally rehearse the lesson and ask:

* How will I engage students?
* How will I make the content come alive?
* How will I ensure the lesson is memorable?

These questions show that good teaching is planned, not improvised.

### 3. Getting into a good emotional state

Jensen says the teacher’s emotional state matters because students notice positive energy. A teacher in a good emotional state creates a better learning experience.

**Important implication:**
The teacher’s emotions affect the classroom climate. Brain-based teaching requires teacher self-management.

### 4. Priming students’ brains with content

The teacher should use the days and weeks before the lesson to expose students to important content. This may include:

* New vocabulary words
* Working models
* Key ideas
* Pictures
* Words posted on classroom walls

This helps students become familiar with content before formal learning begins. 

The slides also mention possible pre-exposure actions such as sending preview tasks home, playing introductory media, and displaying keywords visually before the lesson begins. 

**Exam focus:**
Pre-exposure is not full teaching. It is **priming** the brain so that later learning feels more familiar and easier to connect.

---

## 5. The Physical Learning Environment

Jensen explains that teachers may not be able to control everything about the classroom environment. Therefore, teachers should focus on what they can control and let go of what they cannot control. 

The physical environment matters because it can support or reduce learning. The slide deck describes the goal of this phase as creating a brain state that is **safe and curious**. 

### Key environmental factors:

### 1. Safety

Teachers should promote feelings of safety. Students should feel:

* Physically secure
* Emotionally secure

**Why this matters:**
If students feel unsafe, their brain may focus on protection rather than learning.

### 2. Kinesthetics and movement

Teachers should incorporate kinesthetic opportunities. This includes:

* Flexible seating
* Movement
* Rearranging seats when possible

The slides also list kinesthetic opportunities as part of preparing the space. 

### 3. Temperature

Jensen recommends keeping the classroom temperature between:

**68–72°F**

This detail is very testable because it is specific. 

### 4. Visual environment and lighting

Jensen says brighter lighting is better for most students.

### 5. Acoustics

Teachers should monitor sound. If there is no sound system, the teacher can:

* Move around the room while speaking
* Use the voice theatrically
* Prime students for key directions

### 6. Portable personalization

In some school systems, students stay in one room and teachers move from class to class. If the teacher cannot fully control the room, Jensen suggests bringing items such as:

* Music through a portable CD player
* Posters
* Placards

The teacher can also move or rearrange seating when possible. 

**Exam focus:**
The physical environment is part of brain-based teaching because learning is affected by safety, movement, temperature, lighting, sound, and classroom arrangement.

# Stage 2: During Class / During Learning — 80%

This is the largest part of the model. Jensen says the during-class stage is the most complex stage. It includes five steps:

1. Engagement
2. Framing
3. Acquisition
4. Elaboration
5. Memory strengthening

The book’s Figure 11.1 also includes **Connect**, meaning learning should be connected to other content, processes, and the self. 

The slide deck calls this phase **“The Cognitive Staircase”** because the steps build on each other. 

---

## 6. Step 1: Engagement — The Attentional Hook

Engagement is about activating both the **mind and body**. Jensen says physical or emotional activation prepares students for learning. It may increase:

* Heart rate
* Cortisol
* Dopamine
* Epinephrine

depending on the event. 

The slide deck defines engagement as **active neuronal activation**, not passive listening. Its purpose is to get learners emotionally invested from the beginning. 

### Main principle: learning is state dependent

Jensen says that unless students are in a good emotional state for learning, the teacher has no more important job. He states that **all learning is state dependent**. This means students’ emotional and physical condition affects how well they learn. 

**Very important implication:**
If a teacher ignores students’ emotional states, the teacher is only dealing with the effects of problems, not their causes.

### How teachers can engage students

Jensen suggests using the first few minutes of class to improve students’ states. Possible methods include:

* Journaling
* Humor
* Affirmations
* Art
* Group rituals
* Activities
* Stretching

The slide deck adds that teachers can use targeted resources immediately, such as humor, art, or directly rhythmic group activities, to wake up the nervous system. 

### Social climate

Jensen says teachers should create a positive social climate because students are strongly influenced by peers. This means engagement is not only individual; it is also social. 

**Exam focus:**
Engagement is not just “making the lesson fun.” It means preparing the emotional and physical state of the learner so the brain is ready to learn.

---

## 7. Step 2: Framing — Designing Relevance

After students are awake, alert, and aroused, the teacher moves to framing.

Framing means giving learning a meaningful “frame” so students understand why it matters. Jensen describes framing as the “spin” placed on a word, activity, assignment, or even a disaster. It shapes how students emotionally and mentally approach what follows. 

The slide deck says framing hooks the learner mentally by activating specific neuronal networks. 

### What framing does

Framing:

* Activates neuronal assemblies or networks
* Influences emotional states
* Creates an intentional bias toward what comes next
* Creates an emotional invitation to learn
* Hooks curiosity and wonder
* Helps reduce resistance, demotivation, and boredom

### Examples of framing

A teacher can frame learning by:

* Giving meaning: explaining exactly why something is important
* Using real-life stories
* Using visual metaphors
* Creating background for an activity
* Connecting abstract content to something tangible

The slide deck shows framing as a “relevance frame” that turns abstract complex content into vested interest and neural activation. 

**Exam focus:**
Framing is different from engagement.
**Engagement** wakes up the learner emotionally and physically.
**Framing** gives the learning meaning and relevance.

---

## 8. Step 3: Acquisition — Facilitating Input

Acquisition is the stage where students receive or experience new learning. Jensen says acquisition is happening all the time because the world, the classroom, the teacher’s language, the environment, and social influences all teach students implicitly. 

However, in the formal teaching model, acquisition refers to the explicit instructional input stage.

### Acquisition can happen through:

* Activities
* Experiences
* Field trips
* Guest speakers
* Simulations
* Research
* Lectures
* Reading

Jensen says research, lectures, and reading can be used, but they are not ideal if they are passive. Ideally, acquisition should include active components. 

The slide deck describes the teacher as an **architect of experience** who decides whether learning is best achieved individually or socially. 

### Individual and social learning

Jensen says the teacher must decide whether learning is better done:

* Socially
* Individually

Because the real world includes both, cooperative or collaborative learning may be suitable close to half of the time. 

### Practical acquisition strategies from the slides

* Organize a highly structured activity or experience.
* Bring in a guest speaker for novelty.
* Use experiments, simulations, and role plays.
* Use collaborative learning, pair tasks, and group tasks. 

**Exam focus:**
Acquisition is not simply teacher explanation. It is the input stage, and the best input is often active, experiential, and sometimes social.

---

## 9. Step 4: Elaboration — The Error-Correction Loop

Elaboration means deepening learning through:

* Integration
* Trial and error
* Feedback
* Error correction
* Active processing

Jensen says elaboration helps students develop more comfort and confidence in their learning. 

### Main purpose of elaboration

The teacher must check whether students have:

* Deep understanding
* Accurate understanding

This is important because students often do not know whether they have learned something correctly.

### Why early correction matters

Jensen explains that synaptic connections from acquisition begin solidifying in the first hour after learning. Therefore, the sooner error correction happens, the less fixed the incorrect connection is. Mistakes are easier to correct earlier than later. 

The slide deck emphasizes the same idea as a “core biological insight”: mistakes are easier for the brain to correct earlier in the learning process than later. 

### Working memory limitation

Jensen also warns that students have serious limits in short-term or working memory. They often reduce facts into the “gist” of things. This gist may be:

* Accurate
* Inaccurate

Mental or physical models can reveal whether students misunderstood the material. 

### Error-correction strategies

Jensen lists several strategies:

* Partner quizzes
* Checklists
* Peer editing
* Presentations with feedback
* Competitions

The slide deck also mentions peer editing checklists and training students to give compliments, specific suggestions, and corrections. 

**Exam focus:**
Elaboration is highly testable because it links brain biology to classroom practice:
**Correct errors early because synaptic connections are still forming and are less fixed.**

---

## 10. Step 5: Memory Strengthening

After students’ knowledge becomes accurate, the teacher should help students remember the right content. Jensen criticizes the traditional idea that memory work should be left only to students. Effective teachers help students recall the material. 

### Why timing matters

Jensen says learners develop recall more in the first hour after learning than in the next few days. Therefore, the teacher should use this time wisely. 

The slide deck also states that memory is significantly enhanced when learning is reviewed soon after initial acquisition. It adds that knowledge must be accurate, and teachers must use time wisely to ensure correct encoding. 

### Memory strengthening strategies

Jensen suggests:

* Drama
* Sharing with a partner
* Testing mental models
* Acronyms
* Student-developed visual representations
* Partner reviews
* Quizzes
* Rhymes

The slides add:

* Visual representations
* Mnemonic devices
* Quick review games
* Immediate low-stakes quizzes
* Students teaching the core concepts to peers 

**Exam focus:**
Memory strengthening happens after accurate understanding. It is not enough for students to “understand once”; they need immediate recall support.

---

## 11. Connect: Linking Learning to Other Content, Processes, and Self

In Figure 11.1, Jensen includes **Connect** as part of the during-learning process. Connect means students should link new learning to:

* Other content
* Processes
* The self 

**In-between-the-lines implication:**
Learning becomes stronger when it is not isolated. Students should see relationships between ideas, skills, experiences, and their own lives.

**Example:**
A reading lesson should not only teach vocabulary. It can connect vocabulary to students’ lives, previous texts, grammar, speaking activities, and future writing tasks.

# Stage 3: After Class / After Learning — 10%

The final stage protects and strengthens learning after the main lesson. It includes:

1. Settling time and rest
2. Review and revision 

The slides call this phase **“Settling: The Biology of Passive Processing.”** 

---

## 12. Settling Time and Rest

Jensen says evidence strongly supports **interval learning** over **massed learning**. This means spaced learning with breaks is better than forcing too much information at once. 

The key phrase from Chapter 3 is:

**“Too much, too fast, it won’t last.”** 

### Why settling matters

After learning, students need time for content to settle. The slide deck explains that the brain needs passive processing time to physically build memory structures. Continuous input without rest can cause cognitive drop-off. 

### Settling strategies

Jensen suggests:

* Breaks
* Walks
* Lunch
* Ending class/day with an important learning task so the brain can process it afterward
* Short naps for younger students, because naps can prevent task-performance decline after new learning 

The slide deck adds:

* Quiet reflection time
* Short breaks after intensive lessons
* Scheduling cognitively demanding lessons before lunch or recess
* Light stretching
* Active mindfulness before moving to new subjects 

**Exam focus:**
Settling time is not wasted time. It is part of learning because the brain consolidates information during rest and passive processing.

---

## 13. Review and Revision

By this stage, synaptic connections have been made, but they are not permanent or fixed. Jensen explains that these connections are constantly modified. They can change because of:

* New dissonant content
* Erosion
* Memory deterioration
* Warping of memories 

### Teacher assumption

Jensen says teachers should assume that students:

* Will not get everything right the first time
* Will not remember everything they learned
* Will not remember everything they got right
* Will experience memory deterioration or distortion

He says teachers will be right more often than wrong if they assume this. Therefore, teachers should include revision time every day. 

### Synapses are not static

A major idea is that synapses constantly adapt in response to activity. This means learning must be revisited and used. 

The slide deck connects this to the **forgetting curve** and says unsupported memories will fade or change. Revisiting content strengthens neural pathways. It recommends a spaced review protocol:

* Immediate review: start the next lesson by asking “What do you remember?”
* Spaced review: review after 1 day, 1 week, and 1 month
* Rehearsal: students revise old notes or apply new examples to previous frameworks 

**Exam focus:**
Review and revision are necessary because learning is dynamic. Memories can fade, change, or become distorted without support.

# 15. Continuous Architecture of Learning

The final slide explains that brain-based teaching is **not a fixed, linear formula**. It is an **unbroken cycle**. The memory consolidation strategies used after class become similar to the pre-exposure priming needed before the next complex topic. 

This means:

* “After learning” is not the end.
* Review prepares students for future learning.
* Settling and revision can become priming for the next lesson.
* Brain-based teaching should be used across the curriculum, not only in one activity.

This matches Jensen’s chapter summary: brain-based teaching belongs **in every part of the curriculum**. 

**Exam focus:**
Brain-based teaching is cyclical. The “after” stage of one lesson can become the “before” stage of the next lesson.

# 16. Key Terms and Meanings

## Brain-based teaching

Teaching that uses what is known about the brain and learning to guide lesson planning before, during, and after class.

## Pre-exposure

Introducing students to key ideas, vocabulary, models, or processes before formal teaching begins.

## Priming

Preparing the brain to recognize or connect with upcoming content.

## Engagement

Activating learners emotionally and physically so they are ready to learn.

## Framing

Giving learning relevance, meaning, and emotional importance.

## Acquisition

The input stage where students gain knowledge, skills, values, or experiences.

## Elaboration

Deepening learning through active processing, integration, feedback, and error correction.

## Memory strengthening

Helping students encode and recall learning soon after acquisition.

## Settling

Allowing time for passive processing and consolidation after learning.

## Review and revision

Revisiting and using learning so memories do not fade, distort, or weaken.

## Interval learning

Learning spaced over time with breaks and review.

## Massed learning

Trying to learn too much at once without enough rest or spacing.

# 17. Likely Exam Questions and Model Answers

## Q1. What are the three stages of Jensen’s brain-based teaching model?

Jensen’s model has three stages: before class, during class, and after class. Before class takes about 10% of the learning structure and includes preparing learners and creating an optimal environment. During class takes about 80% and includes engagement, framing, acquisition, elaboration, connection, and memory strengthening. After class takes about 10% and includes settling, rest, review, revision, rehearsal, and incorporation.

---

## Q2. Why does Jensen say there is no perfect way to teach?

Jensen says there is no perfect or best way to teach because many students have been taught successfully using different methods throughout history. Brain-based teaching is not a single universal formula. Instead, it is a model that helps teachers align teaching with research about the brain and mind.

---

## Q3. Explain pre-exposure and give examples.

Pre-exposure means preparing students’ brains before formal learning begins. Teachers can expose students to new vocabulary, key ideas, working models, pictures, preview tasks, introductory media, and wall displays. This makes later learning more familiar and easier to connect.

---

## Q4. Why is the physical environment important in brain-based teaching?

The physical environment affects students’ readiness to learn. Students need to feel physically and emotionally safe. Teachers should also consider movement, seating, temperature, lighting, and sound. Jensen recommends a classroom temperature of 68–72°F and says brighter lighting is better for most students.

---

## Q5. What does Jensen mean by “all learning is state dependent”?

It means students’ emotional and physical state affects learning. If students are stressed, bored, unsafe, or disengaged, they may not learn effectively. Therefore, the teacher must use the first few minutes of class to improve students’ states through humor, journaling, rituals, stretching, art, or other activating strategies.

---

## Q6. What is framing, and why is it important?

Framing means giving learning a meaningful emotional and mental context. It is the “spin” placed on an activity, word, assignment, or problem. Framing helps students see why learning matters, activates neuronal networks, reduces boredom or resistance, and creates curiosity.

---

## Q7. What is the difference between acquisition and elaboration?

Acquisition is the input stage where students gain new knowledge, skills, values, or experiences. Elaboration happens after acquisition and deepens learning through feedback, integration, trial and error, and error correction. Acquisition introduces learning; elaboration checks and strengthens understanding.

---

## Q8. Why should mistakes be corrected early?

Mistakes should be corrected early because synaptic connections begin solidifying soon after acquisition. The earlier the teacher allows error correction, the less fixed the wrong connection is. This makes mistakes easier to correct earlier rather than later.

---

## Q9. Why is memory strengthening important immediately after learning?

Learners develop recall more in the first hour after learning than in the next few days. Therefore, teachers should use immediate review, partner sharing, drama, quizzes, acronyms, rhymes, visual representations, or peer teaching to make sure the correct content is remembered.

---

## Q10. What does “Too much, too fast, it won’t last” mean?

It means that forcing too much content quickly without time for rest, settling, and review weakens learning. The brain needs passive processing time. Interval learning is better than massed learning because spacing and rest support consolidation.

---

## Q11. Why are review and revision necessary?

Review and revision are necessary because synaptic connections are constantly changing. Memories can fade, become distorted, or be changed by new information. Teachers should assume students will not remember everything correctly and should include revision every day.
