# 1. Big Idea of the Chapter

Jensen explains that schools and society value **thinking skills** and **intelligence**, but the way to develop them is not always clear.

The chapter’s main argument is that **critical thinking is brain-based**, meaning it depends on how different brain systems develop, connect, adapt, and respond to experience.

There is **no single exact method** for developing thinking skills. Brain research does not tell teachers exactly what to do step by step. However, it gives useful principles that can guide teaching.

> [!IMPORTANT] Main exam idea
> Critical thinking does not develop through one simple teaching strategy. It develops through repeated, supported, meaningful, varied, and emotionally safe learning experiences.

---

# 2. Thinking vs. Intelligence

Jensen makes a clear distinction:

* **Thinking** = the **process**
* **Intelligence** = the **product**

This means that thinking is what students do, while intelligence is the outcome of effective thinking.

Teachers teach thinking skills so learners can make **more intelligent decisions**.

## Why Do We Teach Thinking Skills?

We teach thinking skills because students need to:

* Solve problems
* Make decisions
* Evaluate information
* Transfer learning
* Use knowledge intelligently
* Respond to new situations
* Think more deeply and independently

> [!EXAM] Likely question
> **What is the difference between thinking and intelligence?**
> Thinking is the process, while intelligence is the product. We teach thinking skills so learners can make more intelligent decisions.

# 3. Lower-Order Brain Systems and Critical Thinking

Jensen explains that **all cognition** is built from lower-order brain systems.

These include:

* **Sensory and motor systems**
* **Auditory and language systems**
* **Attention and executive functions**
* **Social and emotional systems**
* **Memory systems**
* **Behavioral and reward systems**

Students are not born with a complete “smart package.” These lower-order systems must be developed and connected so they can work together at higher levels.

Jensen describes this as helping the brain develop **cross-modality** or **cross-platform functionality**.

## What Does Cross-Platform Functionality Mean?

It means different brain systems work together across different types of tasks.

For example, when a student solves a word problem in mathematics, they are not only using “math ability.” They may also need:

* Reading comprehension
* Working memory
* Attention
* Emotional control
* Language skills
* Problem-solving strategies
* Motivation
* Ability to organize information

> [!IMPORTANT] Key idea
> Critical thinking is not located in only one “thinking part” of the brain. It depends on many brain systems working together.

# 5. The Unique Brain

## Meaning

The **unique brain** means that every learner’s brain is different.

These differences come from:

* **Prenatal differences**
  Things that happen before birth.

* **Postnatal experiences**
  Experiences that happen after birth.

Even if two students perform the same activity, their brains may not activate in exactly the same way.

Some areas of the brain show little variation between people, while other areas vary more noticeably.

## Brain Areas That May Vary

The slide diagram shows variation in areas such as:

* Frontal lobes
* Motor cortex
* Sensory cortex
* Visual cortex
* Auditory cortex
* Reading areas

This means that learners may approach the same task differently because their brain development and experiences are different.

---

## Factors That Cause Brain Differences

Jensen lists several factors that may lead to brain differences:

* **Gender**
* **Exposure to abuse**
* **Exposure to neglect**
* **Specific disorders**
* **Culture**
* **Exposure to drugs**
* **Trauma**
* **Toxins**

These factors can affect how students:

* Learn
* Think
* Respond emotionally
* Solve problems
* Process information
* Understand cause and effect
* Transfer learning

---

## Teaching Implication

Because students’ brains are different, teachers should **not** teach thinking skills using only one approach.

Teachers should use:

* Variety
* Choice
* Different learning activities
* Clear explanations
* Visual support
* Hands-on learning
* Repetition when needed
* Explicit links between ideas
* Support for different learning needs

## Example

A student with:

* Fetal alcohol syndrome
* Prenatal drug exposure
* Head trauma
* Brain injury

may need:

* More repetition
* More explicit instruction
* More hands-on learning
* Clear cause-and-effect pictures
* Stronger teacher support

A student without these difficulties may infer relationships more naturally.

> [!EXAM] Likely question
> **Should teachers use a one-size-fits-all approach to teaching thinking? Why or why not?**
> No. Students’ brains are unique because of prenatal differences and postnatal experiences. Teachers should use variety, choice, accommodations, repetition, and different learning methods.

# 6. Gender-Based Brain Differences

## Important Caution

Jensen discusses gender differences, but this topic must be handled carefully.

The safest way to understand this section is:

* Some biological and developmental differences exist between male and female brains.
* These differences may influence learning and behavior.
* However, teachers should avoid stereotypes.
* Not every boy or girl fits the general pattern.

> [!WARNING] Avoid stereotypes
> The chapter does **not** mean “all boys learn this way” or “all girls learn that way.” It means gender may be one factor among many that influence learning.

---

## Physical and Functional Differences Mentioned

Researchers have found differences in:

* Mean and median brain size, even when adjusted for body size
* Developmental schedules
* Cross-hemispheric connections
* Emotional processing
* Language areas of the brain

The chapter also mentions that first-grade girls are often more ready to read than first-grade boys, but this is a general pattern, not a rule for every child.

---

## Competency Differences Mentioned

### Females may often show strengths in:

* Recalling words
* Labeling objects quickly
* Verbal memory
* Verbal fluency
* Remembering landmarks
* Remembering people
* Matching pictures and letters quickly
* Perceptual speed
* Calculating quantities faster
* Fine-motor tasks

### Males may often show strengths in:

* Throwing targets accurately
* Hitting targets accurately
* Gross-motor tasks
* Reasoning through math problems
* Remembering distances
* Remembering objects

The chapter also mentions that in higher-level math competitions, males outnumber females by about 10 to 1.

However, this does **not** mean all males are better at math or all females are weaker at math. It is presented as a pattern that may involve biological, social, educational, and developmental factors.

---

## Classroom Behavior Patterns

Jensen gives some general classroom observations:

* Boys may be more physical when frustrated.
* Boys may be more physical when communicating a need.
* Boys may be more impulsive.
* Boys may get called on more quickly in class.
* Girls may use language more often to meet their needs.
* Girls may use more covert behaviors.
* Girls may be more cooperative.
* Girls may also be more likely to socialize.

---

## Practical Teaching Suggestions

Teachers should:

* Avoid one-size-fits-all approaches.
* Design balanced learning activities.
* Be mindful of engagement and attention.
* Make simple accommodations.
* Give students with delays more time.
* Give students with delays more repetition.
* Use explicit instruction.
* Provide more classroom support.
* Avoid rigid gender stereotypes.

> [!EXAM] Key answer
> Gender may influence learning and behavior, but teachers should respond with flexible support, not stereotypes.

* The need is compelling.

## How Students Learn Thinking Skills

Thinking skills require:

* **Motivation** to use the skill
* **Role modeling**
* A visible, tactile, or audible model
* **Direct instruction**
* Opportunities to acquire the skill
* **Trial and error**
* Practice
* Debriefing
* Time to use the skill in different contexts

---

## Why Critical Thinking Takes Time

Critical thinking skills take time because learning them changes the brain.

The brain changes in:

* Cortical organization
* Interregional connectivity
* Synaptic connections

In simple words, learning thinking skills literally reorganizes parts of the brain.

This is why quick exposure is not enough.

> [!EXAM] Likely question
> **Why do critical thinking skills take time to learn?**
> Because they require changes in brain organization, new synaptic connections, and repeated practice across time and contexts.

# 8. Intervals and Duration of Thinking-Skills Training

Jensen says teachers should limit the amount of new thinking-skill training at one time.

The suitable duration depends on:

* Complexity of the skill
* Novelty of the task
* Learner’s emotional state
* Age of the learners

## Suggested Training Guidelines

| Learner group  | Suggested duration                   |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Grades K–2     | 20–30 minutes, 2 or 3 times per week |
| Grades 3–5     | Up to 30 minutes, 3 times per week   |
| Grades 6–12    | Up to 60 minutes, 3–5 times per week |
| Adult learners | 45–90 minutes, 3 or 4 times per week |

Many improvements may take:

* Weeks
* Months
* Years

Thinking skills should not be treated as a one-day lesson.

> [!IMPORTANT] Key idea
> Critical thinking training needs repeated exposure over time.

# 9. Context and Transfer of Learning

## Meaning

Thinking skills are often **context dependent**.

This means students may perform well in one setting but fail to use the same skill in a different setting.

For example, a student may understand a skill in class but fail to use it in real life.

---

## Why Transfer Is Difficult

Learning may drop in a new environment because:

* The new environment is distracting.
* The new environment is uncomfortable.
* The new environment feels too unfamiliar.
* The brain remembers old learning only with old cues.
* Students may not transfer learned “rules” to the new situation.
* The social situation changes.
* The task looks different even if the skill is the same.

---

## Street Vendors Example

Jensen gives the example of adolescent street vendors in Brazil.

They used mathematics very accurately in their real-life selling context. Their accuracy was around 98–99%.

However, when they were tested in a laboratory setting, their accuracy dropped sharply, even though the tasks required similar mathematical skills.

## Meaning of the Example

This does not mean the students lacked mathematical ability.

It means their skill was strongly connected to the real-life context where they learned and used it.

---

## Teaching Implication

Schools should use more real-world learning, such as:

* Field trips
* Simulations
* Role-plays
* Away-from-school activities
* Real-life applications
* Project-based tasks
* Practical classroom problems

> [!EXAM] Likely question
> **Why is transfer of learning difficult?**
> Because learning is often tied to the original context, cues, environment, and social situation. Teachers should help students apply skills in multiple real-life contexts.

# 10. Coherent and Challenging Learning

## Main Idea

New learning creates new synapses, especially when learning is challenging.

However, challenge must be managed carefully.

If the task is too easy:

* The brain may not adapt much.
* Students may not grow.

If the task is too hard:

* Students may become frustrated.
* Students may shut down emotionally.
* Students may stop trying.

Teachers need to manage the level of difficulty.

---

## Three Ways Teachers Can Vary Difficulty

### 1. Varying Learning Resources

Teachers can change:

* Whether students work alone or with friends
* Access to information
* Access to tools
* Time allowed
* Amount of support given

### 2. Varying Learning Expectations

Teachers can change:

* Required quality
* Required depth
* Amount of detail
* Whether the final product is public or private
* How much independence is expected

### 3. Varying Learning Context

Teachers can allow students to work:

* At home
* In class
* Over several days
* Over several weeks
* In different environments

---

The brain does not adapt well to meaningless tasks.

Students need to understand why a skill matters.

Teachers can increase relevance through:

* Student choice
* Real-world applications
* Personal connections
* Project-based learning
* Meaningful classroom problems

Movement supports brain-friendly learning.

Teachers should encourage:

* Walking
* Recess
* Athletics
* Personal workout routines
* Active classroom tasks
* Physical engagement

The key idea is that students are not just “brains sitting in chairs.”

The body and brain are connected.

---

## 3. Repetition

Repetition is essential for:

* Learning new skills
* Strengthening neural connections
* Supporting brain reorganization
* Improving automaticity
* Developing confidence

However, repetition should not only mean boring copying.

It can include repeated practice in different:

* Formats
* Tasks
* Contexts
* Examples
* Levels of difficulty

---

## 4. Specificity

Skill practice should move from:

1. A specific subskill
2. Generalizing that subskill
3. Applying it to real-life experiences

Good skill-building activities include:

* Sorting
* Grouping
* Rearranging
* Building
* Organizing
* Assembly
* Designing
* Publishing
* Art projects
* Drama
* Mime
* Theater
* Role-play

* Industrial arts

Feedback and debriefing are necessary.

Without feedback, students may complete the activity but fail to develop clear thinking from it.

> [!IMPORTANT] Key idea
> Fun activities alone are not enough. Students need reflection, feedback, and debriefing to turn activity into thinking development.

# 12. The Maturing Brain

## Main Idea

Different areas of the brain mature at different rates.

Environmental factors can also influence brain maturation.

Brain maturation is not only a passive biological process. Experience matters.

When children are directed to notice and respond to certain stimuli, some brain systems may help “tutor” or develop other brain areas.

---

## Brain Areas Mature at Different Rates

The prefrontal cortex has a peak of synaptic development around ages 1–2.

However, the frontal lobes continue maturing slowly and may not fully mature until around ages 25–30.

This matters because the frontal lobes are very important for critical thinking.

---

## Changes in the Thinking Brain During Maturation

As students mature, they develop stronger abilities in:

* Making inferences
* Theory of mind
* Perceptual functions
* Social awareness
* Working memory
* Language skills
* Reading skills
* Inferring cause and effect
* Concrete thinking
* Abstract thinking

---

## Frontal-Lobe Functions

The frontal lobes are important for:

* Planning
* Judgment
* Decision making
* Working memory
* Critical thinking
* Rule formation
* Mental control
* Persistence

Jensen explains that life experiences slowly shape, prune, and tune this brain area.

---

## Classroom Implication

A wide range of student performance may be due to brain maturation.

A student may not be refusing to think. Their brain may not yet be ready for the level of:

* Abstraction
* Mental control
* Planning
* Delayed gratification
* Working memory
* Emotional regulation

---

## How Teachers Can Support Frontal-Lobe Development

Teachers can support frontal-lobe development through tasks requiring:

* Delayed gratification
* Mental juggling
* Persistence
* Planning
* Careful construction
* Reflection

Useful classroom activities include:

* Arts
* Music
* Performance
* Drawing
* Computer tasks
* Delicate experiments
* Constructing something
* Building something

---

## Important Teaching Point

Sometimes a teacher may teach something several times, and only later does the student understand it.

This may happen because the brain has become more ready.

In other cases, the teacher may only “plant the seeds” for a task and return to it later.

> [!EXAM] Likely question
> **How does the maturing brain affect critical thinking?**
> As the brain matures, especially the frontal lobes, students become better at planning, judgment, working memory, decision making, inference, abstraction, and delaying emotional reactions.

The brain is highly influenced by postnatal experiences.

The interaction between the learner and the environment shapes thinking skills.

This is connected to **neuroplasticity**.

---

## Neuroplasticity

**Neuroplasticity** means the brain can change and adapt because of:

* Experience
* Practice
* Stimulation
* Environment
* Feedback
* Repeated use of skills

---

## How the Adaptive Brain Helps Critical Thinking

The adaptive brain helps students:

* Revise beliefs
* Change strategies
* Respond to new evidence
* Become open-minded
* Develop new pathways for learning
* Improve through experience
* Adjust to new situations

This is central to critical thinking because critical thinkers must be willing to adjust their ideas when evidence changes.

> [!EXAM] Likely question
> **What is the adaptive brain?**

# 16. The Emotional Brain

## Main Idea

Critical thinking depends heavily on emotional states.

Students cannot think well if their emotional state prevents:

* Attention
* Calmness
* Confidence
* Persistence
* Focus
* Motivation

The emotional brain reminds teachers that cognition and emotion cannot be separated.

---

## Emotional States

Emotional states are patterns of brain activity that last for seconds or minutes.

They are influenced by:

* Internal environment
* External environment
* Physical sensations
* Thoughts
* Feelings
* Classroom atmosphere
* Teacher behavior
* Peer interaction

Emotional states are constantly changing.

A student may move from:

* Curiosity
* Boredom
* Anxiety
* Hunger
* Confidence
* Frustration
* Joy

These states affect learning and thinking.

---

## Stable States

If students repeatedly enter the same emotional state, the brain may become more likely to return to that state later.

Examples:

* A student who often becomes angry may reactivate anger more easily.
* A student who often feels anxious may return to anxiety quickly.
* A student who practices focus may become better at returning to focus.
* A student who repeatedly experiences determination may develop determination as a stable state.
* A student who repeatedly experiences creativity may become more able to enter a creative state.

> [!IMPORTANT] Key implication
> Teachers can help students build useful emotional states through repeated positive learning experiences.

---

## Sources of Emotional States

Emotional states draw from three things:

### 1. Sensations

Examples:

* Hunger
* Cold
* Itchiness
* Tiredness
* Physical discomfort
* Sunburn
* Feeling bloated

### 2. Mental State

Examples:

* Confidence
* Scattered thinking
* Calculating
* Alertness
* Confusion
* Focus

### 3. Feelings

Examples:

* Disappointment
* Joy
* Disgust
* Sadness
* Excitement
* Anger

Students may experience several things at the same time, but they can consciously focus on only one aspect of an emotional state at a given moment.

---

# 17. Compartmentalizing

## Meaning

To **compartmentalize** means to set aside negative or distracting parts of an emotional state so the learner can focus on what needs to be done.

It allows students to downplay the negative aspects of a state and activate a more useful state.

## Examples

A student may feel hungry or sad but still activate determination to complete a task.

A student may feel nervous before a presentation but focus on confidence and preparation.

A student may feel frustrated during a difficult question but choose to stay calm and continue working.

---

## Why Compartmentalizing Matters

Compartmentalizing is important because it helps students:

* Focus
* Continue working
* Avoid giving up
* Manage negative emotions
* Complete difficult tasks
* Think clearly under pressure
* Move forward even when uncomfortable

This is one of the most important skills for success in life.

---

## Where Students Learn It

Students may learn compartmentalizing from:

* Home
* Sports
* Hobbies
* Outdoor challenge programs
* School

If students do not learn it outside school, teachers may need to help them develop it.

> [!EXAM] Likely question
> **Why is compartmentalizing important for critical thinking?**
> It allows students to reduce the effect of negative emotional states and activate useful states such as confidence, focus, or determination.

---

# 18. How Teachers Can Support Emotional State Management

Students first need to recognize their emotional states.

If students cannot identify their state, they cannot manage it effectively.

One example from the chapter is a classroom chart called:

> “How am I feeling today?”

This type of chart helps students name and notice what they feel.

---

## How Teachers Influence Emotional States

Teachers can influence students’ emotional states through:

* The questions they ask
* Classroom movement
* Student posture
* Activities used in class
* Personal encouragement
* Teacher attitudes toward students
* Teacher opinions of students
* Respect
* Affirmation
* Hobbies and habits teachers support
* Learning successes students experience

Even small changes can shift emotional states, such as:

* Sitting up straight
* Changing breathing
* Choosing a new thought
* Moving posture
* Receiving encouragement

---

## Practical Activities for Emotional Awareness

Activities that help students develop emotional awareness include:

* Drama
* Theater
* Role-play
* Journaling about feelings
* Identifying feelings from a list
* Discussing emotional states with peers
* Discussing emotional states with adults
* Discussing emotions of literary characters

---

## Strategies for Younger Students

For pre-K to grade 3, teachers can use simple cues.

### Traffic Light Example

| Cue          | Meaning                                            |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Green light  | Continue with active learning                      |
| Yellow light | Slow down, pause, think, or talk quietly           |
| Red light    | Stop, freeze, wait for directions, or ask for help |

This teaches young children that they can control their behavior and emotional responses.

---

## Strategies for Older Students

Older students can explore how different factors affect their thinking, such as:

* Food
* Worry
* Excitement
* Anger
* Despair
* Romance
* Stress

A useful strategy is short daily journaling.

### Daily Journal Prompts

Students can write:

1. What happened?
2. How did I feel?
3. What did I do next to influence how I felt?

---

## Lesson Openings and Endings

Teachers should begin lessons with activities that create:

* Receptive states
* Curious states
* Focused states

Teachers should end lessons with positive states, such as:

* Celebration
* Joy
* Achievement
* Reflection

> [!EXAM] Likely question
> **How can teachers help students manage emotional states for critical thinking?**
> By teaching emotional recognition, linking emotions to thinking, using supportive classroom activities, encouraging self-regulation, and creating positive learning states.

Thinking skills develop through:

* Repeated exposure
* Practice
* Support
* Scaffolding
* Maturation
* Experience
* Feedback
* Emotional regulation

Students need more than one lesson on thinking skills. They need repeated opportunities over time.

All learners can benefit, including:

* Children
* Adolescents
* Adults
* Gifted students
* Struggling students

All students need education about thinking skills.

Early childhood or school-based interventions can lead to:

* Better language fluency
* Higher IQ
* Better cognitive processes
* Stronger social intelligence
* Stronger emotional intelligence
* Reduced discipline problems
* Reduced tardiness
* Reduced vandalism

* Reduced academic failure

## Whole-Child Approach

A stressful or economically deprived home environment can reduce the positive effects of thinking-skills programs.

Therefore, schools should use a **whole-child approach**.

This means considering:

* Home environment
* Community
* Nutrition
* Siblings
* After-school programs
* Emotional support
* Enrichment opportunities

---

## Budget-Cut Warning

Jensen warns that schools often cut programs that are actually good for developing intelligence and thinking skills.

These include:

* Field trips
* Vocational education
* Performance arts
* After-school programs
* Lab classes
* Music instruction
* Competitions
* Visual arts
* Project-based learning

From a neurobiological perspective, Jensen argues that brains can be changed for the better.

Educators therefore have an ethical responsibility to support that development.

> We can change brains for the better, and educators have an ethical obligation to do so.

# 20. High-Probability Exam Questions

## 1. What is the difference between thinking and intelligence?

Thinking is the process. Intelligence is the product. Teachers develop thinking skills so students can make more intelligent decisions.

---

## 2. What are the five brain-based aspects that promote critical thinking?

They are:

1. The unique brain
2. The problem-solving brain
3. The maturing brain
4. The adaptive brain
5. The emotional brain

---

## 3. Why should teachers avoid one-size-fits-all teaching?

Because every brain is unique due to prenatal differences and postnatal experiences.

Students differ because of:

* Gender
* Culture
* Disorders
* Trauma
* Abuse
* Neglect
* Toxins
* Life experiences

Teachers should use variety, choice, accommodations, repetition, and different learning methods.

---

## 4. Why do critical thinking skills take time to learn?

Because they require changes in:

* Brain organization
* Synaptic connections
* Communication between brain regions
* Cortical organization
* Interregional connectivity

They also require repeated practice over time.

---

## 5. Why is transfer of learning difficult?

Because learning is often strongly tied to context.

Students may perform well in one setting but fail to apply the same skill in another setting because the environment, cues, and social situation are different.

---

## 6. What supports coherent learning?

The four supports are:

1. Relevance
2. Activity
3. Repetition
4. Specificity

---

## 7. How does the maturing brain affect student performance?

Different brain areas mature at different rates.

The frontal lobes, which are important for planning, judgment, decision making, working memory, and critical thinking, mature slowly.

This means some student difficulties may be due to brain maturation rather than laziness or refusal.

---

## 8. What is neuroplasticity?

Neuroplasticity is the brain’s ability to change and adapt because of experiences, environment, practice, and stimulation.

---

## 9. Why are emotional states important for critical thinking?

Because students must manage emotions in order to:

* Focus
* Think clearly
* Persist
* Stay motivated
* Make good decisions
* Avoid impulsive reactions

---

## 10. What does compartmentalizing mean?

Compartmentalizing means setting aside distracting or negative emotional states and activating a useful state, such as focus, confidence, or determination.

---

## 11. What classroom activities can improve emotional awareness?

Activities include:

* Drama
* Theater
* Role-play
* Journaling
* Identifying emotions from a list
* Discussing emotional states
* Discussing characters’ emotions in reading materials

---

## 12. Why are enrichment programs important?

They support:

* Thinking skills

* IQ
* Cognitive processes
* Social intelligence
* Emotional intelligence
* Discipline
* Academic success
* Long-term positive outcomes

The chapter argues that critical thinking is developed through the interaction of many brain systems.

Students need:

* Direct teaching of problem-solving skills

* Real-world contexts
