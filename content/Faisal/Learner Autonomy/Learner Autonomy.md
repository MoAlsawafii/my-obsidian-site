## 1. Introduction: Why Autonomy Matters in Education

**Autonomy** has become an important concept in education, especially since the last century. It is linked to the idea that learners should not only receive knowledge from teachers, but should also participate actively in their own learning.

Three major thinkers influenced the theory and practice of learner participation in education:

1. **Paulo Freire**
2. **Ivan Illich**
3. **Henri Holec**

### Core Idea

Modern education increasingly values autonomy because learning is no longer seen as simply memorizing information.

Instead, learners are expected to:

* make decisions,
* think critically,
* reflect on their progress,
* take responsibility,
* connect learning to real life,
* continue learning beyond the classroom.

> [!exam] Likely Exam Questions
>
> * Why has autonomy become important in modern education?
> * How is learner autonomy different from traditional teacher-centered education?
> * How do Freire, Illich, and Holec support learner autonomy?

---

# 2. Freire and the “Banking” Model of Education

## Freire’s Criticism of Traditional Education

Freire criticized the idea of education as **“banking.”**

In the banking model:

* learners are treated like **empty containers**;
* the teacher “deposits” information into them;
* students receive, file, store, and repeat information;
* the teacher instructs and imposes ideas;
* learners are passive rather than active.

The diagram on page 3 shows this idea clearly: information is poured into the learner as if the learner is a passive container. This represents **instruction and imposition**, not real learning.

---

## Meaning of the Banking Paradigm

The banking paradigm means:

* the teacher controls knowledge;
* students are expected to memorize;
* learners do not question or construct meaning;
* learning becomes mechanical;
* students depend heavily on teachers.

### Example in an Omani Educational Context

Possible examples include:

* students waiting for the teacher to give exact exam answers;
* memorizing textbook content without questioning;
* expecting teachers to explain everything step by step;
* studying only what will come in the exam;
* depending on teacher notes rather than reading independently.

---

## Freire’s Link to Autonomy

Freire’s work supports learner autonomy because he believed that people should be aware of:

* their own actions,
* the consequences of their actions,
* how their actions affect themselves,
* how their actions affect others and society.

> [!important] Key Idea
> Learning is something learners **do**, not something that is **done to them**.

This means that students are not passive objects. They are active participants in learning.

---

## Autonomy as Freedom and Responsibility

Freire connects autonomy with both:

* **freedom**, and
* **responsibility**.

Learners should have freedom to think and act, but they must also accept responsibility for their learning choices.

> [!exam] Exam Answer
> Freire rejected the “banking” model because it makes learners passive. His view supports autonomy because learners should be active, aware, and responsible participants in their own learning.

# 3. The Crisis of Educational Dependency

The image on page 3 also presents **“The Crisis of Educational Dependency.”**

## The Symptom: Spoon-Feeding

According to Al-Saadi, spoon-feeding creates several problems:

* students assume teachers are responsible for all classroom events;
* it continues dependency rather than self-direction;
* learners study only for exams;
* students cannot take initiative even after many years of formal education.

---

## Important Implication

If students spend more than 13 years in school but still cannot take initiative, the problem is not only the student.

The educational system itself may have trained them to be dependent.

The slide suggests that the educational system may accidentally weaken learners by preventing them from controlling their own learning.

This connects strongly with both **Freire** and **Illich**.

> [!exam] Likely Exam Questions
>
> * What is spoon-feeding?
> * How does spoon-feeding reduce autonomy?
> * What is the relationship between exam-focused learning and dependency?

# 4. Illich and the Critique of Institutional Dependency

## Illich’s Main Criticism

Illich criticized the dependency that the educational system creates.

He argued that education often makes people believe:

* only teachers can teach;
* only institutions can provide learning;
* learners learn only because teachers teach;
* real learning happens only in schools or universities.

---

## Problem with This Belief

This belief weakens learners because it prevents them from controlling their own learning.

Learners become dependent on:

* teachers,
* classrooms,
* institutions,
* official curricula,
* exams,
* certificates.

---

## Illich’s Proposal

Illich proposed:

* liberation of education;
* preservation of autonomy;
* increased reliance on self-learning;
* an educational society where learning happens through autonomous events.

---

## Meaning of “Autonomous Events”

Autonomous learning events are learning experiences that happen because learners choose to engage, explore, interact, or investigate, not only because an institution forces them to.

### Examples

* learning English through podcasts independently;
* practicing speaking with peers outside class;
* using online resources for self-study;
* choosing personal learning goals;
* joining a language community.

> [!exam] Freire vs Illich
>
> * **Freire** criticized the teacher-centered “banking” model.
> * **Illich** criticized dependency on teachers and institutions.
> * Both support the idea that learners should have more control and responsibility.

# 5. Holec’s Definition of Autonomy

Holec is one of the most important names in learner autonomy.

## Holec’s Definition

> **Autonomy is the ability to take charge of one’s own learning and to hold responsibility for all decisions concerning all aspects of that learning.**

---

## Learners’ Responsibilities According to Holec

Autonomous learners are responsible for:

### 1. Determining Objectives

Learners decide what they want to learn.

This means they set their own learning goals.

### 2. Defining Content and Progression

Learners decide:

* what content to study;
* the order of learning;
* the pace of learning.

### 3. Selecting Methods and Techniques

Learners choose:

* how to learn;
* which strategies to use;
* which tools or activities to use.

### 4. Monitoring the Procedure of Acquisition

Learners check how learning is going.

They notice:

* difficulties,
* progress,
* weaknesses,
* strategy problems.

### 5. Evaluating What Has Been Acquired

Learners judge what they have learned.

They check:

* progress,
* strengths,
* weaknesses,
* whether goals have been achieved.

---

## Example

A language learner who wants to improve speaking may:

* set a goal: “I want to speak more fluently about daily topics”;
* choose content: daily conversations, travel, university life;
* choose methods: speaking with classmates, recording voice notes, using apps;
* monitor progress: noticing pronunciation problems;
* evaluate learning: comparing recordings over time.

> [!exam] Memorize This
> Holec’s definition is highly testable:
>
> **Autonomy is the ability to take charge of one’s own learning.**
>
> Also memorize the five decision areas:
>
> 1. objectives,
> 2. content/progression,
> 3. methods/techniques,
> 4. monitoring,
> 5. evaluation.

# 6. Autonomy in Language Learning and Teaching

## Shift in Language Teaching

Language teaching has moved away from only teaching:

* grammar,
* vocabulary,
* isolated language forms.

It now prioritizes:

* communicative functions,
* individual needs,
* social norms,
* autonomy,
* learning as interactive and social.

---

## Meaning

Language learning is not only about knowing grammar rules.

It is also about:

* communicating effectively;
* using language in real situations;
* understanding social use of language;
* adapting to learner needs;
* learning through interaction.

> [!important] Important Implication
> Autonomy in language learning is not simply private study.
>
> It also includes communication, collaboration, reflection, and social awareness.

# 7. Terms Related to Autonomy but Not the Same

Autonomy is often used with related terms, but these terms are not identical.

The related terms are:

* **individualization**,
* **self-instruction**,
* **self-direction**,
* **independent learning**,
* **self-regulation**,
* **self-access**.

The diagram on page 8 presents these on a continuum from teacher control to learner control.

---

## Key Idea from the Diagram

Some concepts look autonomous, but they still involve teacher control.

True learner autonomy is closer to the learner-control side because it involves:

* conscious capacity,
* willingness,
* metacognitive ability,
* control over one’s own learning.

---

# 8. Individualization

## Definition

**Individualization** means instruction is adapted to the learner’s needs and pace by the teacher or system.

According to Dickinson, the teacher is involved with the learning process but not directly controlling every part of it.

It is adapted to an individual by considering that learner’s characteristics.

---

## Example from the Slides

A teacher gives:

* Student A a list of basic vocabulary;
* Student B a list of advanced idioms;

based on a placement test.

---

## Why This Is Not the Same as Autonomy

The instruction is personalized, but the teacher still makes the main decisions.

The student may not decide:

* objectives,
* content,
* pace,
* methods,
* evaluation.

> [!note] Simple Distinction
> **Individualization** = learning is adapted **for** the learner.
> **Autonomy** = learning is controlled **by** the learner.

> [!exam] Likely Question
> How is individualization different from autonomy?
>
> **Answer:** Individualization may consider learner needs, but decisions are still usually made by the teacher or system. Autonomy requires learners to take responsibility and make decisions about their own learning.

---

# 9. Self-Instruction

## Definition

**Self-instruction** means learning without the direct control or physical presence of a teacher.

According to Dickinson, deciding to learn without a teacher means the learner, alone or with others, works without direct teacher control.

---

## Main Features

Self-instruction focuses on:

* the learning setting;
* the structural mode of learning;
* whether the teacher is physically present;
* learning without direct teacher control.

---

## Important Correction

Self-instruction does **not automatically mean autonomy**.

A learner may study alone but still follow a plan completely created by someone else.

---

## Example

A student studies an online grammar course alone at home.

This is self-instruction because no teacher is physically controlling the learning.

However, if the student simply follows fixed instructions without choosing goals, strategies, or evaluation, it may not be full autonomy.

> [!exam] Key Difference
> **Self-instruction** is about the absence of teacher control.
> **Autonomy** is about ability, willingness, responsibility, and choice.

---

# 10. Self-Direction

## Definition

**Self-direction** is an attitude toward learning where the learner accepts responsibility for learning, but may not carry out learning actions independently.

According to Dickinson:

> The learner accepts responsibility for learning, but does not necessarily carry out courses of action independently.

---

## Important Point

A learner can be self-directed while still attending a normal teacher-led class.

---

## Example from the Slides

A student thinks:

> “I need to improve my pronunciation, so I will ask my teacher for extra practice materials.”

This is self-directed because the student recognizes a need and takes responsibility.

However, the action still depends on the teacher.

---

## Difference from Autonomy

Self-direction is part of autonomy, but autonomy is broader.

Autonomy includes:

* responsibility,
* decision-making,
* planning,
* monitoring,
* evaluating,
* willingness,
* capacity.

Self-direction mainly emphasizes the learner’s attitude of accepting responsibility.

> [!exam] Likely Question
> Can a learner be self-directed in a teacher-led class?
>
> **Answer:** Yes. A learner may accept responsibility and make purposeful choices even within a conventional classroom.

---

# 11. Independent Learning

## Definition

**Independent learning** is the act of working alone on a task.

---

## Important Warning

Working alone does not necessarily mean the learner is autonomous.

According to Ryan and Deci, merely doing independent learning does not mean the learner consciously decided to do it.

---

## Example from the Slides

A student is told to complete a worksheet quietly at their desk without help.

This is independent work, but not necessarily autonomy.

---

## Why?

Because the student may have:

* no choice,
* no control,
* no personal goal,
* no responsibility for planning,
* no involvement in evaluation.

> [!note] Simple Distinction
> **Independent learning** = working alone.
> **Autonomy** = consciously taking control.

> [!exam] Very Likely Exam Point
> Learners instructed to work independently cannot automatically be called autonomous.

---

# 12. Self-Regulation

## Definition

**Self-regulated learning** involves psychological processes used to control learning.

According to Zimmerman, it includes managing:

* motivation,
* attention,
* strategies.

---

## Relationship to Autonomy

In some views, learner autonomy is similar to self-regulated learning.

Learner autonomy involves reflective involvement in:

1. planning,
2. implementing,
3. monitoring,
4. evaluating learning.

---

## Why Self-Regulation Is Important

Autonomous learners need self-regulation because they must control:

* how they study,
* when they study,
* how focused they are,
* whether their strategy is working,
* how they respond to difficulty.

---

## Example

A learner preparing for an English speaking exam may:

* plan daily speaking practice;
* use pronunciation videos;
* monitor fluency;
* evaluate recordings;
* change strategies if progress is slow.

> [!exam] Memorize
> The four key processes are:
>
> 1. planning,
> 2. implementing,
> 3. monitoring,
> 4. evaluating.

---

# 13. Self-Access

## Definition

**Self-access** is a methodology where students have access to a physical or digital space with materials organized for independent study.

Examples:

* a resource room,
* a language lab,
* a website,
* an online learning platform,
* a library section,
* a digital learning center.

---

## Difference Between Self-Access and Autonomy

Self-access provides the **tools and opportunity**.

Autonomy is the **skill and will** to use those tools effectively to direct one’s own learning.

---

## Example

A university may provide an English learning website.

That is self-access.

But students are autonomous only if they:

* choose useful materials,
* set goals,
* monitor learning,
* evaluate progress,
* use the resources responsibly.

> [!exam] Likely Comparison
> **Self-access** is the availability of resources.
> **Autonomy** is the ability and willingness to use resources to control learning.

# 14. Common Misconceptions About Autonomy

Little identified five common misconceptions about autonomy.

The table on page 15 contrasts the misconception with the operational reality.

---

## Misconception 1: Autonomy Means Self-Instruction

Some people think autonomy means simply working without a teacher.

### Reality

Autonomy is not simply studying alone.

A learner can be autonomous inside a classroom, and a learner can be non-autonomous while studying alone.

---

## Misconception 2: Teachers Must Give Up All Control

Some believe teachers must surrender control to promote autonomy.

### Reality

Autonomy can exist in classrooms where teachers still maintain structure and pedagogical control.

Teachers do not disappear.

They guide, support, and structure learning.

---

## Misconception 3: Autonomy Is a New Methodology

Some believe learner autonomy is a teaching method that can be programmed into lesson plans.

### Reality

Autonomy is not a fixed method or a series of lesson plans to follow blindly.

It is a complex educational goal and process.

---

## Misconception 4: Autonomy Is One Simple Behavior

Some think autonomy is a single, easily described behavior.

### Reality

Autonomy takes many forms depending on:

* age,
* stage of learning,
* goals,
* context,
* learner ability,
* subject area.

---

## Misconception 5: Autonomy Is a Permanent State

Some believe once learners become autonomous, they stay autonomous forever.

### Reality

Autonomy fluctuates.

A learner may be autonomous in one area but dependent in another.

For example:

* autonomous in vocabulary learning;
* dependent in grammar learning;
* autonomous in university study;
* dependent in exam preparation.

> [!exam] Highly Likely Question
> Mention Little’s misconceptions about autonomy.
>
> Best answer: list the five misconceptions and correct each one.

# 15. Dependent Learners vs Independent Learners

The diagram on page 18 compares dependent and independent learners across five bands.

---

## Band 1: Reliance

### Dependent Learner

* relies heavily on the teacher.

### Independent Learner

* is self-reliant.

---

## Band 2: Decision-Making

### Dependent Learner

* cannot make decisions about learning.

### Independent Learner

* makes informed decisions about learning.

---

## Band 3: Real-World Connection

### Dependent Learner

* does not connect classroom learning with the real world.

### Independent Learner

* connects learning to real-world contexts.

---

## Band 4: Goal Setting and Motivation

### Dependent Learner

* works only for extrinsic rewards, such as grades.

### Independent Learner

* is intrinsically motivated;
* plans learning;
* sets goals.

---

## Band 5: Reflection

### Dependent Learner

* is unaware of strengths and weaknesses;
* does not reflect.

### Independent Learner

* regularly reflects on process and progress.

> [!important] Important Point
> Independence is not only “working alone.”
>
> It includes:
>
> * decision-making,
> * real-life connection,
> * motivation,
> * goal setting,
> * reflection.

# 16. Definitions of Autonomy

## Benson’s Definition

Benson defines autonomy as:

> **The capacity to take control over one’s own learning.**

---

## Degrees of Autonomy

Autonomy is not all-or-nothing.

Different learners show different degrees of autonomy because of differences in:

* capacity,
* opportunity,
* motivation.

---

## Meaning

A learner may want to be autonomous but may lack opportunity.

Another learner may have opportunity but lack motivation.

Another may have both but still lack skills.

So autonomy depends on:

1. **capacity to take control**,
2. **opportunities for taking control**,
3. **motivation to take control**.

> [!exam] Memorize
> Benson’s definition:
>
> **Autonomy is the capacity to take control over one’s own learning.**
>
> Also remember that autonomy exists in degrees, not as a fixed state.

---

# 17. Operational Definition of Autonomy

## Problem

The literature does not fully agree on one general definition of learner autonomy or how it can be observed.

This means autonomy is complex and difficult to measure.

---

## Important Clarification

Lack of one definition does not mean autonomy is meaningless.

It can still be:

* practiced,
* researched,
* promoted,
* analyzed.

---

## Sinclair’s Framework

Sinclair provides useful aspects of learner autonomy that help integrate different meanings.

The diagram on page 21 presents the **“Anatomy of Autonomy”** as four pillars.

---

# 18. Four Foundational Pillars of True Learner Autonomy

## 1. Capacity and Willingness

Autonomy requires the ability to make informed decisions combined with active willingness to take responsibility.

This means a learner needs both:

* skill,
* motivation.

A learner may have the ability but not the willingness.

Another learner may be willing but not know how.

---

## 2. Metacognition

Metacognition means conscious awareness of the learning process.

It includes:

* planning,
* setting goals,
* self-assessment,
* exploiting resources.

This is central because autonomous learners think about how they learn.

---

## 3. Social Dimension

Autonomy is not isolation.

It relies on:

* social interaction,
* collaboration,
* awareness-raising with peers.

Autonomy can happen through working with others.

---

## 4. Contextual Nuance

Autonomous behavior varies across cultures and contexts.

It is influenced by:

* political aspirations,
* policies,
* cultural conventions,
* educational traditions.

> [!exam] Strong Answer
> A strong answer on operational autonomy should mention:
>
> * autonomy can happen inside and outside class;
> * it has individual and social dimensions;
> * it has psychological and political dimensions;
> * it depends on context.

---

# 19. Autonomy Inside and Outside the Classroom

According to Sinclair, autonomy can happen both inside and outside the classroom.

## Key Features

Learner autonomy involves:

* planning,
* experimenting,
* reviewing,
* making decisions.

The setting is not the most important factor.

The important factor is whether learners have opportunities to make meaningful decisions.

---

## Example Inside Class

Students choose which article to read and decide how to present their understanding.

---

## Example Outside Class

Students choose English podcasts to improve listening and reflect on progress.

---

# 20. Social Dimension of Autonomy

Autonomy has both:

* an individual dimension,
* a social dimension.

---

## Social Autonomy

Social autonomy recognizes that learning and awareness happen through:

* interaction,
* collaboration,
* discussion,
* peer support,
* group reflection.

> [!important] Important Implication
> Autonomy does not mean “learning alone.”
>
> It can include learning with:
>
> * peers,
> * teachers,
> * native speakers,
> * family members,
> * community members.

---

# 21. Political and Psychological Dimensions

## Psychological Dimension

This relates to the learner’s internal processes, such as:

* motivation,
* confidence,
* self-regulation,
* decision-making,
* reflection.

---

## Political Dimension

This relates to control, power, and decision-making in education.

Examples include:

* selecting learning materials;
* pair or group work;
* discussing personal goals;
* discussing experiences of language learning;
* planning educational policy for language learning.

---

## Important Implication

Autonomy is not only about individual motivation.

It is also affected by who has power to decide:

* what is learned,
* how it is learned,
* how it is evaluated.

---

# 22. The Fluctuation of Autonomy

The graph on page 23 shows that autonomy fluctuates over time and context.

## Main Idea

Autonomy is a pursuit, not an absolute standard.

It is not stable all the time.

---

## High Autonomy May Occur When Learners Have

* high language competence,
* positive affective mood,
* optimal environmental conditions.

---

## Low Autonomy May Occur When Learners Experience

* psychological fatigue,
* high task difficulty,
* negative attitudes toward materials.

---

## Key Insight from the Graph

Degrees of autonomy are unstable.

They fluctuate because of a complex interaction of:

* affective variables,
* environmental variables,
* physiological variables.

---

## Examples

A student may feel autonomous when studying vocabulary using an app but feel dependent when writing academic essays.

A learner may be highly motivated one week but lose autonomy during exam stress or fatigue.

> [!exam] Likely Question
> Is autonomy a permanent state?
>
> **Answer:** No. Autonomy fluctuates across time, task, context, motivation, competence, mood, and environment.

---

# 23. Summary of the Concept of Autonomy

The slides state that no single definition with observable characteristics can fully describe autonomy.

However, autonomy is still meaningful because the related concepts help analyze the roles of:

* learners,
* teachers,
* senior administrators,
* educational institutions.

---

## Important Conclusion

Autonomy is complex, multi-dimensional, and context-dependent.

It involves:

* learner capacity,
* willingness,
* decision-making,
* responsibility,
* reflection,
* social interaction,
* institutional support,
* cultural and political context.

---

# 24. Learning Autonomy and the Roles of Learners, Teachers, and Institutions

Researchers differ on:

* where autonomy comes from;
* how autonomy develops;
* who is responsible for promoting it.

Autonomy is shaped by:

* learners,
* teachers,
* institutions.

---

## Benson’s Sources of Restraint

Benson identifies several restraints on autonomy:

* government,
* educational institutions,
* conceptions of language,
* conceptions of language learning,
* language teaching methodologies.

---

## Meaning

Learners and teachers do not operate freely.

They work inside systems that may support or restrict autonomy.

---

# 25. Ecosystem of Restraint and Support

The diagram on page 26 shows overlapping spheres of influence.

## Main Areas

### 1. Institutional and Policy Factors

These include:

# 27. Learners’ Roles in Autonomy

Learners contribute to their own autonomy in two main ways:

1. their will or motivation to act;
2. the actions they decide to take by exercising autonomy.

---

## Learner-Based Restrictions

Restrictions may come from learners’ own beliefs about:

* language;
* what language learning is like;
* what language itself is like;
* the idea of “correct” language use.

---

## Examples

If a learner believes English is only grammar rules, they may avoid communicative practice.

If a learner believes only native-like pronunciation is correct, they may feel anxious and avoid speaking.

If a learner believes the teacher must explain everything, they may not try self-study strategies.

# 28. How Learners Can Minimize Constraints

Sturtridge suggests learners can be trained to minimize constraints by learning how to:

1. recognize unsuitable materials;
2. use materials productively;
3. be aware of different types of materials;
4. perceive the purpose of tasks;
5. evaluate tasks;
6. use peers and others as resources.

---

## Explanation of Each Point

### 1. Recognizing Unsuitable Materials

Learners should know when materials are:

* too easy,
* too difficult,
* irrelevant,
* outdated,
* not useful for their goals.

### 2. Using Materials Productively

Learners should not only read or complete tasks mechanically.

They should know how to benefit from materials.

### 3. Awareness of Different Types of Materials

Learners should understand that language learning materials may include:

* textbooks,
* videos,
* podcasts,
* graded readers,
* websites,
* dictionaries,
* conversation partners,
* authentic texts.

### 4. Perceiving Task Purpose

Learners should know why they are doing a task.

For example:

* vocabulary matching develops word recognition;
* listening repetition develops pronunciation and comprehension;
* speaking practice builds fluency.

### 5. Evaluating Tasks

Learners should decide whether a task helped them or not.

### 6. Using Peers and Others as Resources

Autonomous learners do not depend only on the teacher.

They can learn from:

* classmates,
* friends,
* native speakers,
* online communities,
* family members,
* tutors.

# 33. Affective Strategies

## Definition

Affective strategies help learners deal with emotional issues involved in language learning.

They involve managing:

* vulnerability,
* anxiety,
* fear of mistakes,
* self-esteem,
* confidence,
* motivation.

---

## Why They Matter

Learners may feel vulnerable or deficient when learning a language.

## Example

A learner who feels embarrassed speaking English may use affective strategies such as:

* accepting mistakes,
* setting small achievable goals.

# 35. Cognitive Strategies

## Definition

Cognitive strategies use the learner’s mental skills to facilitate language learning.

* grouping words,

# 36. Metacognitive Strategies

## Definition

Metacognitive strategies are used for:

* monitoring,

# 42. Breen and Mann’s Teacher Requirements

Breen and Mann call on teachers of autonomous learners to:

1. know their beliefs about teaching and learning;
2. reflect on how their decisions affect each learner;
3. assume that each learner is able to learn and capable of taking an autonomous stance;
4. adapt their practice to foster learner autonomy.

---

## Explanation

### 1. Know Their Beliefs

Teachers should understand their own assumptions.

For example:

* Do they believe students can make decisions?
* Do they believe mistakes are part of learning?
* Do they believe the teacher must control everything?

### 2. Reflect on Decisions

Teachers should think about how classroom choices affect students.

For example:

* Does this activity give learners responsibility?
* Does this exam encourage memorization only?
* Does this feedback help learners reflect?

### 3. Believe Learners Are Capable

Teachers should not assume students are naturally dependent.

They should treat students as capable of developing autonomy.

### 4. Adapt Practice

Teachers should be flexible and change teaching methods to support learner autonomy.

# 43. Learner Autonomy and Self-Determination Theory

Self-Determination Theory, or **SDT**, explains motivation and psychological growth.

According to SDT, people need three things:

1. autonomy,
2. competence,
3. connection or relatedness.

The image on page 41 shows these as humans’ three basic needs leading to motivation.

---

# 44. Autonomy in SDT

## Definition

People need to feel in control of their own behaviors and goals.

This sense of being able to take direct action that leads to real change helps people feel self-determined.

---

## Connection to Learner Autonomy

When learners feel they have some control over learning choices, they become more motivated.

Examples of choice:

* topics,
* tasks,
* methods,
* materials,
* project format,
* study strategies.

---

# 45. Competence in SDT

## Definition

People need to gain mastery of tasks and learn different skills.

When people feel they have the skills needed for success, they are more likely to take actions to achieve their goals.

---

## Connection to Learner Autonomy

Learners are more willing to take responsibility when they believe they can succeed.

Teachers can support competence by:

* giving clear instructions,
* scaffolding tasks,
* providing feedback,
* building skills gradually,
* helping students see progress.

---

# 46. Connection or Relatedness in SDT

## Definition

People need a sense of belonging and attachment to other people.

The slide also calls this **connection** or **relatedness**.

---

## Connection to Learner Autonomy

Learners are more motivated when they feel supported by:

* teachers,
* classmates,
* peers,
* learning communities.

This again shows that autonomy is not isolation.

---

# 47. Connection Between SDT and Learner Autonomy

The slides state:

* when teachers give choices, students feel more autonomous;
* when students feel autonomy, they become more motivated;
* this leads to better engagement, independence, and deeper learning.

---

## Important Chain

**Teacher choice-giving → student autonomy → motivation → engagement → independence → deeper learning**

> [!exam] Likely Short-Answer Question
> How does teacher choice support learner autonomy according to SDT?
>
> **Answer:** Choice helps students feel autonomous. This increases motivation, which improves engagement, independence, and deeper learning.

# 48. SDT and Accountability

The slides clarify that SDT does not reject accountability.

Instead, SDT supports using the principles of:

* autonomy,
* competence,
* relatedness,

to achieve autonomous motivation.

---

## Important Implication

Autonomy does not mean students can do whatever they want with no responsibility.

Autonomy can exist with accountability.

Students can be responsible for learning outcomes while still having meaningful choices and support.

> [!warning] Do Not Say This in the Exam
> Do not say autonomy means no rules or no accountability.
>
> That is incorrect.

# 56. Final Big Ideas

Learner autonomy is not simply learning alone.

It is a complex capacity involving:

* responsibility,
* decision-making,
* motivation,
* reflection,
* strategy use,
* awareness of the learning process.

Autonomy can happen:

* inside the classroom,
* outside the classroom,
* with a teacher,
* without a teacher,
* individually,
* socially.

It depends on the learner’s capacity and willingness, but also on:

* teachers,
* institutions,
* policies,
* culture,
* assessment,
* available opportunities.

A truly autonomous learning environment does not remove teachers or institutions.

Instead, it redesigns their roles so that learners gradually become more capable, reflective, motivated, and responsible.
