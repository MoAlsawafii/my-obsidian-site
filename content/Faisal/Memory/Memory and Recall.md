# 1. Big Idea of the Chapter

Memory and recall are central to learning because teachers can only know that students have learned something when students can **recall** it and demonstrate it.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Learning is not proven just because the teacher taught something. Learning is only visible when students can **retrieve and show** what they learned.

Jensen explains that teachers have only **three main opportunities** to influence learning:

1. **Original encoding**

   * This is the moment when information first enters the learner’s brain.
   * Example: hearing an explanation, reading a page, watching a demonstration, doing an activity.

2. **Memory maintenance**

   * This is how the memory is kept alive after initial learning.
   * It includes review, reactivation, correction, repetition, and use.

3. **Memory retrieval**

   * This is the process of bringing the memory back when needed.
   * Example: answering a question, taking a test, explaining a concept, applying a skill.

The chapter’s major message is:

> [!important] Core principle
> **Memories are malleable.**
> This means memories are flexible, changeable, and can be strengthened, weakened, distorted, corrected, or reshaped.

This has a major teaching implication:

* Forgetting is not always a sign that students did not learn.
* Sometimes students learned something but cannot retrieve it.
* Sometimes they retrieve an incomplete or inaccurate version.
* Sometimes the way teachers ask for recall makes students look more forgetful than they really are.

# 2. Correcting Misconceptions About Memory

## The wrong idea: memory is like a video recording

A common misconception is that the brain records life like a camera, DVD, or movie.

According to this mistaken view:

* Experiences are stored exactly as they happened.
* Memory works like replaying a video.
* If we find the right cue, we can replay the whole memory accurately.

Jensen rejects this idea.

## Where this misconception came from

The misconception partly came from the work of **Wilder Penfield**, a Canadian neurosurgeon, in the 1930s and 1940s.

During brain surgery, Penfield electrically stimulated parts of patients’ temporal lobes. Some patients seemed to experience vivid recall, almost like short movie clips.

However, this evidence was weak because:

* These recall episodes happened in fewer than 5% of patients.
* The patients were seizure patients, not healthy random people.
* Later researchers questioned whether the memories were truly accurate.
* Other surgeons did not successfully replicate the same findings.

So, the “brain as a video recorder” idea is not supported.

## The better definition of memory

Memory is better understood as:

> A lasting change in the brain caused by a temporary stimulus.

In simpler words:

* A short experience can create a long-term change in the brain.
* That brain change is what we call memory.

### Example

You hear a phone number for a few seconds.

* The phone number is the **temporary stimulus**.
* Your brain strengthens certain neural connections.
* Later, if those connections activate again, you may recall the number.
* The strengthened connection is the **memory**.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Memory is not a stored movie. It is a changed pattern in the brain that can later be reactivated.

# 3. Survival-Based Memories

## Humans are wired to remember survival-related information

Jensen argues that humans are naturally good at remembering information related to survival.

We easily remember:

* Locations
* Procedures
* Emotional experiences
* Conditioned responses

This is because these types of memories helped humans survive.

---

## 3.1 Locations

Humans remember important places very well.

Examples:

* Where we live
* Where food is found
* Where a hospital is
* Where work or school is
* Where important people are

The brain has strong “where” pathways that map important locations.

This type of memory is connected to:

* Spatial memory
* Episodic memory
* Location-based recall

### Classroom implication

Students may remember learning better when it is connected to a place, movement, or classroom location.

---

## 3.2 Procedures

Humans remember how to do important actions.

Examples:

* Walking
* Eating
* Talking
* Driving
* Cooking
* Getting dressed
* Using tools
* Caring for children

These memories are usually long-lasting and reliable.

### Classroom implication

Learning that involves doing, practicing, or physically performing something is more likely to be remembered than learning through words only.

---

## 3.3 Emotional experiences

Humans strongly remember emotionally powerful events.

Examples:

* Car accidents
* Robberies
* Natural disasters
* Weddings
* Births
* Divorces
* Celebrations
* Being hurt by someone
* Being helped by someone

These memories often last a long time and may not need much review.

### Classroom implication

Teachers can make learning more memorable by adding appropriate emotion, interest, surprise, celebration, or meaningful experience.

---

## 3.4 Conditioned responses

A conditioned response is a learned reaction to a specific stimulus.

Examples:

* Feeling nervous when someone criticizes your driving
* Feeling happy when offered a favorite food
* Reacting to the way someone says your name
* Responding emotionally to a smell, sound, or repeated classroom routine

Conditioned responses become stronger through repetition.

### Classroom implication

Classroom routines, repeated cues, songs, signals, or emotional associations can become automatic memory triggers.

---

## Humans are not naturally wired for school-type memory

Jensen makes an important point:

Humans are not naturally strong at remembering many things commonly taught in school, such as:

* Words
* Names
* Equations
* Isolated facts
* Textbook details
* Lists of information

These are often **semantic memories**, and they are less directly connected to survival.

Examples from the slides:

* Do students really need to remember the names of the world’s seven longest rivers for survival?
* Do they naturally remember names of ancient civilizations and their details?

Usually, no.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Some memories are naturally strong because they are survival-based. School facts are often weak because they are semantic and less survival-related.

### Teaching implication

Teachers should not expect students to automatically remember facts just because they heard them once.

Instead, teachers need to use strategies that connect facts to:

* Emotion
* Context
* Movement
* Meaning
* Repetition
* Multiple pathways
* Review
* Retrieval cues

# 4. Making a Memory

## Memory depends on neural firing and connections

Jensen describes memories as the likelihood that a certain pattern of neurons will fire again.

In simple terms:

* A memory is not stored in one single cell.
* A memory is a pattern across a network of neurons.
* When the same network fires again, recall becomes possible.

Simple memories may involve thousands of neurons.
Complex memories may involve millions of neurons.

---

## The process of creating memory

Memory formation involves electrochemical activity between neurons.

### Step 1: Electrical impulse travels down the axon

An electrical signal moves along the axon of a neuron.

This leads to the release of chemicals called **neurotransmitters**.

The process also involves **messenger ribonucleic acids**, or **mRNAs**, which carry information.

At the same time, **synaptic adhesion** begins.

Synaptic adhesion means protein-like “strings” help bind neurons together at the synapse.

---

### Step 2: Chemicals enter the synaptic gap

Neurotransmitters and mRNAs are released into the space between neurons.

This space is called the **synaptic gap**.

---

### Step 3: Chemicals dock into receptor sites

The chemicals attach to receptor sites on the receiving neuron’s dendrite.

This changes the receiving neuron.

---

### Step 4: Long-term potentiation happens

When the electrochemical signal is strong enough, **long-term potentiation**, or **LTP**, occurs.

LTP means the connection between neurons becomes stronger because it has been used.

> [!important] Key definition
> **Long-term potentiation** is a use-dependent strengthening of synaptic connections.

---

### Step 5: A new electrical signal is created

The receiving dendrite sends new electrical activity toward the cell body.

This helps create or strengthen the memory network.

---

## Learning equals stronger connections

Learning happens when connections between neurons are strengthened.

This strengthening can be affected by:

* Repetition
* Emotion
* Attention
* Stress hormones
* Neuromodulators
* Sensory input
* Sleep
* Meaningful processing

> [!important] Exam idea
> Memory formation is not just “information entering the brain.” It involves physical and chemical changes in neural connections.

---

# 5. Distribution of Memory in the Brain

## There is no single memory storage location

A very important point in this chapter is that memories are not stored in one single place.

There is no one “memory box” in the brain.

Instead:

* Different parts of an experience are processed in different brain areas.
* Different kinds of memories use different systems.
* A single classroom lesson may activate many memory pathways.

---

## Sleep improves encoding

Jensen explains that most encoding is improved by a good night’s sleep.

The more complex the learning is, the more helpful sleep becomes.

### Teaching implication

Students need sleep to consolidate learning, especially complex learning.

---

## Memories are stored where they were first processed

In general, memories are encoded in the areas that originally processed them.

Examples:

| Type of information       | Brain area involved                  |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Visual memories           | Occipital lobe                       |
| Language memories         | Temporal lobe                        |
| Spatial memories          | Parietal lobe                        |
| Learned skills            | Cerebellum and striatum              |
| Classroom facts           | Prefrontal cortex and temporal lobes |
| Number sense              | Left parietal lobe                   |
| Higher math skills        | Parietal and temporal lobes          |
| Emotional events          | Amygdala                             |
| Explicit/spatial memories | Hippocampus                          |

---

## Important brain structures

### Amygdala

The amygdala helps process and remember emotional events.

It is especially involved in memories connected to:

* Fear
* Disappointment
* Horror
* Joy
* Celebration

---

### Hippocampus

The hippocampus helps form and retrieve explicit memories, especially:

* Spatial memories
* Episodic memories
* Speaking
* Reading
* Emotional event recall

---

### Cortex

The temporal lobes are important for semantic retrieval.

Semantic retrieval includes facts, words, and textbook-type information.

---

### Cerebellum

The cerebellum is involved in:

* Procedural learning
* Reflexive learning
* Conditioned responses

---

### Prefrontal cortex

The prefrontal cortex supports working memory.

However, long-term memory is not simply “stored” there in one fixed place.

---

### Parietal lobes

The parietal lobes are involved in:

* Short-term memory activation
* Spatial processing
* Some number sense

---

### Body memories

The slides also mention that some memories may be stored in peptide molecules that circulate through the body via the bloodstream.

This supports the idea that memory is not only a “head” process; the body can be part of memory.

---

## Memory is reconstructed

Because memory is distributed, recall requires reconstruction.

For example, in a classroom lesson:

* What students saw may be stored in one area.
* What they heard may be stored in another area.
* What they felt may be stored in another system.
* What they physically did may activate another pathway.

When students recall the lesson, the brain must reconstruct these separate pieces into a meaningful whole.

Jensen compares this to putting fragmented pieces back together.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Recall is reconstruction, not replay.

---

## Brain injury and uneven memory

Because memory is distributed, brain injury can affect one type of memory while leaving another type intact.

Example:

* A student may retrieve common nouns but struggle with proper nouns.
* A student may remember procedures but not facts.
* A student may perform unevenly across subjects.

### Teaching implication

If a child shows unusual or very specific memory patterns, teachers should refer the child to a specialist rather than simply assuming laziness or low ability.

# 6. Memories’ Multiple Pathways

Different experiences are encoded in different ways, so they have different chances of being recalled.

The more pathways teachers activate, the better chance students have of retrieving learning later.

> [!important] Core teaching implication
> If learning is only presented through words, recall is weak. If learning is connected to visuals, movement, emotion, context, discussion, and practice, recall becomes stronger.

---

## Two broad categories of memory

Memory can be divided into two major categories:

1. **Explicit memory**
2. **Implicit memory**

These are also called:

| Common term     | Alternative term      |
| --------------- | --------------------- |
| Explicit memory | Declarative memory    |
| Implicit memory | Nondeclarative memory |

---

## Explicit memory

Explicit memory is conscious memory.

It includes information we can usually explain, describe, or declare.

Explicit memory includes:

1. **Semantic memory**

   * Words
   * Facts
   * Symbols
   * Textbook information
   * Abstract ideas
   * Pictures and videos

2. **Episodic memory**

   * Personal experiences
   * Locations
   * Events
   * Contexts
   * “Where were you when...?” memories

Explicit memory also includes short-term and working memory.

---

## Implicit memory

Implicit memory is less conscious and more automatic.

It includes:

1. **Procedural memory**

   * Skills
   * Habits
   * Body learning
   * Hands-on learning
   * Bicycle riding
   * Manipulatives

2. **Conditioning**

   * Automated learning
   * Learned responses

3. **Reflexive memory**

   * Simple cognitive reflexes
   * Flashcards
   * Repetition-based automatic responses

4. **Emotional intensity**

   * Trauma
   * Pleasure
   * Strong emotional experiences

5. **Sensory reflexive memory**

   * Hot stove effect
   * Favorite objects
   * Triggered responses

6. **Priming**

   * Perceptual or subconscious cues
   * Remembering something because a cue activated it indirectly

---

# 7. Semantic Memory

## Definition

Semantic memory is a type of explicit memory.

It is also known as:

* Declarative memory
* Factual memory
* Taxon memory
* Linguistic memory

Semantic memory includes:

* Names
* Facts
* Figures
* Textbook information
* Words
* Symbols
* Abstract ideas
* Information from lectures
* Information from reading
* Information from visual aids
* Information from videos or DVDs
* Information from conversations

---

## Why semantic memory is weak

Semantic memory is one of the weakest retrieval systems.

This is a major problem because school depends heavily on semantic memory.

Examples of semantic learning:

* Listening to a lecture
* Reading a textbook
* Memorizing vocabulary
* Learning definitions
* Learning dates
* Learning formulas
* Learning names of theories

Students often retain little of what is said in lectures.

Jensen gives an example of a study where college students who attended lectures knew only slightly more than those who skipped.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Lecture-based learning is weak because it depends heavily on semantic memory, which has limited time and capacity.

---

## Working memory and short-term memory

Semantic memory is limited by working memory.

Working memory refers to the short-term online maintenance and manipulation of information.

In simple words:

* It is what the learner can hold in mind right now.
* It usually lasts only a short time.
* It disappears unless it is processed, repeated, or connected to meaning.

The slides explain short-term memory as an active neural firing pattern.

An idea is in short-term memory only while the firing pattern is active.

---

## Time limitation

An item in working memory may last around **5 to 30 seconds** unless it is reactivated.

Example:

* You meet someone and forget their name seconds later.
* You read a page and realize your mind went blank.
* A teacher gives several instructions and students forget half of them.

---

## Capacity limitation

Working memory capacity is limited.

Older theories suggested people could hold around seven items, plus or minus two.

Jensen argues that this is too optimistic for classroom use.

A more practical classroom view:

* Students under 12 often handle one item at a time.
* Students older than 12 may handle two or three things on a good day.
* Many learners should still receive only one instruction at a time.

Students with attention difficulties, learning delays, or auditory-processing difficulties may have even lower working memory capacity.

---

## Chunking

Chunking means grouping separate pieces of information into meaningful units.

Example:

Instead of remembering:

* 9 - 6 - 8 - 2 - 4 - 5 - 1

A learner may remember:

* 968
* 2451

Chunking helps because it reduces the number of separate items the learner must hold.

> [!important] Key term
> **Chunking** = grouping information into meaningful units to reduce working memory load.

---

## Factors affecting working memory capacity

Working memory capacity depends on:

* Rate of input
* Meaning
* Strategy
* Novelty
* Primacy
* Recency
* Age of learner

### Primacy

Learners tend to remember what comes first.

### Recency

Learners tend to remember what comes last.

### Novelty

New, unusual, or surprising information may receive more attention.

---

## Practical teaching suggestions for semantic memory

### 1. Stop after each page

When students read a chapter, they should stop after each page and take notes.

This prevents information from disappearing before it is processed.

---

### 2. Use partner work

One student can read while another maps the content.

This helps because it turns passive reading into active processing.

---

### 3. Pause lectures after a few minutes

If the teacher lectures, students should pair up after a few minutes and reteach the idea to each other.

This supports:

* Rehearsal
* Meaning-making
* Error correction
* Consolidation

---

### 4. Use group correction

If students struggle, the teacher can use a larger group activity to correct misunderstandings.

Example:

* Group discussion with a prompting handout

---

### 5. Give directions one at a time

Instead of saying many instructions at once, teachers should break them down.

Example:

1. “Please stand up.”
2. Pause.
3. “Slide your chairs in gently.”
4. Pause.
5. “When I say go, line up at the door.”
6. Check understanding.
7. “Ready, get set, go.”

---

### 6. Do not remove book learning completely

Jensen does not say schools should remove facts, books, or textbooks.

Students still need:

* Facts
* Directions
* Safety information
* References
* Poetry
* Novels
* Letters
* Textbooks

But teachers must understand that semantic learning needs support.

---

## Ways to strengthen semantic encoding

Teachers can ask students to:

* Compare and contrast the material.
* Summarize what was learned.
* Make a rhyme from key ideas and teach it to a classmate.
* Turn learning into a nonlinguistic representation.

  * Drawing
  * Comic strip
  * Diagram
  * Mind map
* Analyze and critique the material.
* Consider the material from different points of view.
* Group and regroup the material into categories.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Semantic memory is weak unless students actively process meaning.

---

# 8. Episodic Memory

## Definition

Episodic memory is a type of explicit memory.

It is also called:

* Autobiographical memory
* Loci memory
* Spatial memory
* Event-related memory
* Contextual recall

Episodic memory connects learning to:

* Place
* Location
* Personal experience
* Circumstance
* Event context

It creates a kind of map of daily experiences.

---

## The key condition: personal presence

For episodic memory to work strongly, the learner must personally experience the event.

Reading about something is not the same as being there.

### Example: safari

Reading about elephants is semantic memory.

Standing near an angry elephant during a safari is episodic memory because the person personally experienced the event with emotion, location, and sensory input.

---

## Brain areas involved

Episodic memory involves:

* Hippocampus
* Medial temporal lobe

It is supported by:

* Curiosity
* Novelty
* Expectations
* Sensory input

---

## Sensory input strengthens episodic memory

Episodic memories are stronger when they include:

* Sights
* Sounds
* Smells
* Taste
* Touch

This explains why real-life experiences are often easier to remember than textbook descriptions.

---

## Episodic memory has unlimited capacity

Jensen describes episodic memory as:

* Effortless
* Natural
* Used by everyone
* Having unlimited capacity

A common example:

If someone asks, “What did you eat for dinner last night?” you may first ask yourself, “Where was I?”

The location helps trigger the content.

---

## “What” and “where” pathways

The visual system has two pathways:

1. **What pathway**

   * Content
   * Objects
   * Information

2. **Where pathway**

   * Location
   * Spatial context

Every learning experience happens somewhere, so contextual cues can help retrieve learning.

---

## Major drawback: contamination

Episodic memory has one big weakness: **contamination**.

Contamination happens when too many memories are connected to the same location.

Example:

* Same classroom
* Same desk
* Same seat
* Same seating arrangement
* Same textbook
* Same routine every day

The information may be in the brain, but students cannot separate one memory from another because everything has the same “file name.”

### Simple explanation

If students always learn in the same place in the same way, many memories become mixed together.

They may know the material but lack clear retrieval hooks.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Episodic memory is powerful, but if all learning happens in the same context, memories can become contaminated.

---

## Teaching strategies for episodic memory

Teachers should use mobility and varied context.

Examples:

* Stand-up reviews
* Learning stations
* Labs
* Regrouping students
* Going outside
* Trading rooms with another teacher
* Allowing students to stand
* Allowing students to lean against a wall or desk
* Changing seating arrangements
* Connecting topics to different areas of the classroom

The goal is to create distinct contextual cues.

---

# 9. Reflexive Memory

## Definition

Reflexive memory is a type of implicit memory.

It includes automatic responses.

Some reflexes are natural or instinctive.

Examples:

* Jerking your leg when a doctor taps your knee
* Covering your ears when you hear a loud siren
* Blinking when something comes toward your eyes

But Jensen also explains that learned information can become reflexive.

---

## Two kinds of reflexive pathways

Reflexive memory can include:

1. **Emotional memories**

   * Favorite song
   * First kiss
   * Car accident
   * Celebration
   * Embarrassing moment

2. **Nonemotional associative memories**

   * Flashcard responses
   * Repeated facts
   * Automatic answers
   * Rehearsed patterns

---

## Two ways learning becomes reflexive

New learning can become reflexive in only two major ways:

1. **Intense sensory input**

   * Trauma
   * Celebration
   * Emotional events
   * Surprising sensory experiences

2. **Repetition**

   * Practice
   * Overlearning
   * Flashcards
   * Repeated retrieval

> [!important] Exam idea
> Reflexive learning becomes automatic through emotion or repetition.

---

## Classroom examples

### Flashcards

Flashcards can make facts automatic through repeated exposure.

---

### Overlearning

Overlearning means practicing beyond the point of basic understanding until retrieval becomes automatic.

---

### Raps and songs

Students may remember content through raps because:

* Rhythm creates auditory cues.
* Repetition strengthens memory.
* Physical movement may be involved.
* The learning becomes more automatic.

A student who cannot remember textbook content may still remember a content-based rap.

---

### Fill-in-the-blank tests

A blank space can trigger either:

* Semantic retrieval, if the student learned it as a fact.
* Reflexive retrieval, if the student overlearned it through repetition.

---

# 10. Emotions and Memory

## Emotions receive special treatment in memory

Emotional events are more likely to be remembered.

Jensen explains that the stronger the original emotional event, the more likely it is to be retrieved later.

Examples:

* A standing ovation
* A harsh teacher rebuke
* A joyful project celebration
* A scary event
* A surprising classroom moment

---

## Norepinephrine and memory

Norepinephrine, also called noradrenaline, is released during:

* Emergency
* Risk
* Excitement
* Urgency

It acts like a memory fixative.

In simpler words:

* It helps “lock in” exciting or traumatic memories.
* It makes the event more memorable.

---

## Teaching implication

Teachers can and should create appropriate emotional experiences in the classroom.

This does not mean creating fear or trauma.

It means using positive, meaningful emotional engagement.

---

## Ways teachers can link emotion and memory

Teachers can use:

1. **Unusual aromas**

   * Freshly baked cookies
   * Bread
   * Other noticeable smells

2. **Happy occasions**

   * Celebrations
   * Positive rituals
   * Acknowledgments
   * Class achievements

3. **Gross or unusual science materials**

   * Wet displays
   * Bubbly displays
   * Slippery materials
   * Gooey experiments

4. **Storytelling**

   * Suspense
   * Surprise
   * Mystery
   * Emotional narrative

Emotional responses during or immediately after learning help embed memories.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Emotion strengthens memory, especially when it happens during or right after learning.

---

# 11. Procedural Memory

## Definition

Procedural memory is a type of implicit memory.

It is also called:

* Motor memory
* Body learning
* Habit memory

Example:

* Riding a bicycle even after years without practice

---

## Procedural memory is shown through action

Procedural memory appears in:

* Actions
* Behaviors
* Skills
* Movement
* Physical performance

It is activated by:

* Sports
* Dance
* Games
* Theater
* Role-play
* Hands-on activities
* Physical movement

---

## Strengths of procedural memory

Procedural memory usually has:

* Very large storage capacity
* Minimal need for review
* Low dependence on intrinsic motivation
* Strong long-term durability

This makes it very useful for teaching.

---

## Brain and body connection

Jensen emphasizes that the body and brain are not separate.

What happens to the body also affects the brain.

Physical action creates a richer memory map because it adds:

* Movement
* Sensory input
* Body position
* Emotion
* Context
* Visual cues

This makes storage and retrieval easier.

---

## Hands-on learning

Physical activities are highly memorable.

Examples:

* Role-playing
* Science experiments
* Creating projects
* Industrial arts tasks
* Building
* Moving
* Playing
* Trying things out

These are common in early childhood learning.

However, as students get older, schools often reduce hands-on learning.

Jensen argues this is a problem because procedural learning is robust and memorable.

---

## Teaching strategies for procedural memory

Teachers can use:

* Puppets
* Charades
* Manipulatives
* Dance
* Movement
* Theater
* Drama
* Outdoor activities
* Recess activities
* Physical education
* Sports
* Indoor games
* Visual arts
* Music
* Puzzles
* Learning stations

---

## Error correction and celebration

When using procedural learning, teachers should allow time for:

1. Practice
2. Mistakes
3. Correction
4. Feedback
5. Celebration

This helps students remember the correct version of the skill or concept.

---

## Example strategy: three-step movement

If the teacher has three points to teach:

1. Ask students to stand.
2. Ask them to take three steps.
3. Introduce the first point with an action.
4. Students take three more steps.
5. Introduce the second point with an action.
6. Repeat for the third point.
7. Students sit down.

This links content to movement and physical location.

---

# 12. Memory Maintenance

## Why memory needs maintenance

Memories do not stay perfect forever.

Most things people are exposed to are forgotten.

Even when something is remembered, the memory may change.

This is because memories are malleable.

---

## Seven reasons memories fail

Daniel Schacter identified seven major reasons why memory can fail.

---

## 12.1 Transience

Transience means memory fades over time.

If a memory is not used, it becomes weaker.

Example:

* Forgetting a topic learned months ago because it was never reviewed.

---

## 12.2 Absent-mindedness

Absent-mindedness happens when the person was not paying attention during encoding.

Example:

* A student hears instructions but is thinking about something else.

The information may never have been encoded properly.

---

## 12.3 Blocking

Blocking is when the memory feels available but cannot be retrieved.

Example:

* “It’s on the tip of my tongue.”

---

## 12.4 Misattribution

Misattribution happens when the person remembers something but connects it to the wrong source.

Example:

* A student remembers an idea but thinks it came from the teacher when it actually came from a classmate.
* A student remembers the general idea but confuses the details.

---

## 12.5 Suggestibility

Suggestibility happens when outside information contaminates memory.

Example:

* A leading question changes how someone remembers an event.
* A classmate’s wrong explanation affects another student’s memory.

---

## 12.6 Bias

Bias happens when existing beliefs, prejudices, or later knowledge change the memory.

Example:

* A student remembers a group activity more negatively because they already dislike the group.

---

## 12.7 Persistence

Persistence happens when a negative memory keeps returning and affects new learning.

Example:

* A student who failed before keeps remembering that failure and becomes anxious during similar tasks.

---

> [!important] Exam idea
> Memory failure is not only “forgetting.” It can also involve distortion, contamination, bias, or emotional persistence.

---

## Even strong memories can change

Jensen gives examples showing that even memories people think are unforgettable can contain errors.

Examples include memories of major public events such as disasters or famous trials.

The point is:

* Emotional memories feel accurate.
* But feeling certain does not always mean the memory is accurate.

---

## Reactivation and reconsolidation

When a memory is reactivated, it can become unstable again.

Then it must be stored again through reconsolidation.

This means:

* Reactivating a memory can strengthen it.
* But it can also change it.
* If a wrong version is reactivated and not corrected, the wrong memory may become stronger.

---

## Procedural memory is less malleable

Some memories are harder to change than others.

Procedural memories are often very strong.

Example:

Tiger Woods had to change his golf swing. Since the old swing was deeply procedural and automatic, changing it required explicit effort and took a long time.

### Teaching implication

Habits and procedures can be changed, but they need time and practice.

---

## Practical suggestions for maintenance

Teachers should expect that students may:

* Forget
* Remember incorrectly
* Mix up information
* Strengthen wrong memories if not corrected

So teachers should use:

* Daily reviews
* Weekly reviews
* Mind maps
* Paired sharing
* Group discussions
* Error correction
* Feedback
* Repeated retrieval

> [!important] Exam idea
> Review is not just repetition. Review helps correct and reconsolidate memory.

---

# 13. Memory Retrieval

## Encoding and retrieval are different

One set of factors helps create memories.

Another set of factors helps retrieve them.

This means a student may have learned something but fail to retrieve it under certain conditions.

---

## Active memories can be fragile

Memories that were recently formed or recently accessed can be less consolidated.

They may be:

* More fragile
* More changeable
* More difficult to retrieve
* More open to distortion

---

## Synaptic consolidation

Synaptic consolidation happens within minutes to hours after initial learning.

It is the process by which neural changes become more stable.

---

## Short-term memory retrieval requires repetition

Short-term memory is activity-dependent.

This means it needs repeated activation to stay alive.

Without repetition or processing, it fades quickly.

---

## Long-term memory depends on modulators

Long-term memory formation involves:

* Encoding
* Transcription
* Synaptic growth

These processes are modulatory-dependent.

That means they depend on chemicals in the brain, such as neuromodulators.

Neuromodulators are affected by:

* Emotion
* Engagement
* Arousal
* Stress
* Motivation

---

## Brain chemistry and retrieval

Recall can be influenced by:

* Hormones
* Food
* Neurotransmitters
* Chemical arousal
* Sleep-related consolidation

---

## Acetylcholine

Acetylcholine is a neurotransmitter involved in long-term memory formation.

The brain uses acetylcholine to support memory.

---

## Choline

Choline helps produce acetylcholine.

If the brain has more choline, it can better support acetylcholine production.

---

## Lecithin

Lecithin raises choline levels.

Foods containing lecithin include:

* Eggs
* Salmon
* Lean beef

The slides summarize the chain like this:

**Lecithin → choline → acetylcholine → memory and nerve communication**

---

## Other nutrients that support memory

Nutrients associated with memory support include:

* Folic acid
* Lipoic acid
* Vitamin B
* Vitamin C
* Vitamin E

Food sources include:

* Spinach
* Lean proteins
* Most vegetables
* Salmon

---

## Sugar and memory

The slides mention that a small amount of household sugar in the bloodstream can enhance memory if given after a non-emotional learning event.

However:

* Sugar does not affect emotional memories in the same way.
* Emotional memories use different stress-response mechanisms.

> [!important] Exam caution
> Do not overstate this as “sugar is always good for learning.” The point is about chemical effects on recall, not a general nutrition recommendation.

---

# 14. Matching Learning and Testing Conditions

## State-dependent recall

Recall is stronger when the learner is in the same emotional state as when the memory was formed.

Examples:

* Learning while relaxed may be easier to recall when relaxed.
* Learning while stressed may be recalled differently under stress.
* Learning in a happy state may be easier to recall in a similar state.

---

## Problem: learning state and testing state may differ

Students may learn in one emotional condition and test in another.

Example:

* Learning in a relaxed classroom
* Testing under anxiety and time pressure

This mismatch can reduce performance.

---

## Strategy 1: Teach emotional regulation

Teachers can help students manage their test-time emotional states.

Examples:

* Relaxation methods
* Positive self-talk
* Breathing techniques
* Confidence-building routines

---

## Strategy 2: Practice in different states

Teachers can rehearse learning in a variety of emotional states.

This creates **recall resiliency**.

Recall resiliency means students can retrieve learning under different conditions.

Examples:

* Timed quizzes
* Public quizzes
* Small-group presentations
* Practice tests
* Mock exams
* Low-pressure and higher-pressure review formats

---

## Strategy 3: Match location and context

Recall can improve when testing conditions match learning conditions.

This is because the hippocampus creates a spatial map of where learning happened.

Examples:

* Reviewing in the same room where the test will happen
* Using the same classroom context
* Keeping familiar environmental cues
* Using matching sensory cues

---

## Sensory cues

Sensory cues can support recall.

Examples:

* Smell
* Taste
* Visual cues
* Room arrangement
* Location
* Classroom posters

Jensen mentions studies where matching foods, such as chocolate or peppermint, during learning and testing helped recall.

---

# 15. Priming and Wait Time

## Definition of priming

Priming happens when a cue activates a memory, often before the learner is fully aware of it.

Memories may form even when people are not paying full attention.

---

## Incidental priming

Incidental priming is unintentional.

### Slide example

A person sees Burger King while driving but does not consciously pay attention to it.

Later, they suddenly feel like ordering Burger King.

The earlier visual cue primed the memory.

---

## Intentional priming

Intentional priming happens when the teacher deliberately gives cues before learning.

Examples:

* Asking review questions
* Giving hints
* Showing pictures
* Activating prior knowledge
* Mentioning key words
* Giving a partial word cue
* Asking students what they already know

In education, this activates students’ schemata.

---

## Priming and implicit memory

Priming engages implicit memory.

This means students may “know” something without realizing they know it until the cue activates it.

---

## Priming is not cheating

Jensen argues that giving cues is not cheating.

Cues help the brain retrieve what is already there.

Example:

A teacher says:

* “Earlier we discussed that memories are located in different areas of the br____.”

The letters “br” help students retrieve “brain.”

---

## Multiple-choice tests and priming

Students often like multiple-choice tests because the options act as retrieval cues.

This does not always mean they did not learn.

It means the brain sometimes needs prompts for recall.

---

## Wait time

Students need time to retrieve memories.

Their brains may be processing many competing thoughts.

If teachers do not prime students, they should at least give them time to think.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Wait time supports retrieval because activation may need time to reach the required level.

---

# 16. Other Strategies for Improving Memory

## 16.1 Intentional wordplay

Semantic information can be strengthened using:

* Rhymes
* Visualization
* Mnemonics
* Peg words
* Music
* Discussion

Without these strategies, reading can be forgettable.

---

## 16.2 Frequent pauses during reading

Students should stop after every quarter page or half page to:

* Take notes
* Discuss
* Reflect
* Summarize
* Ask questions

This helps transfer learning to long-term memory.

---

## 16.3 Daily and weekly review

Teachers should conduct:

* Oral review
* Written review
* Daily review
* Weekly review

Students can:

* Pair up
* Rotate in teams
* Present daily reviews
* Teach each other

---

## 16.4 Spaced review

Jensen suggests repeating key ideas:

1. Within 10 minutes of original learning
2. Again 48 hours later
3. Again around 7 days later

This supports long-term retention.

> [!important] Exam idea
> Spaced learning with pauses and intervals is better than one long exposure.

---

## 16.5 Acrostics

Acrostics are memory aids where the first letter of each key word forms a phrase or word.

Examples:

* Planet order phrase
* “Every good boy does fine” for music notes
* HOMES for the Great Lakes:

  * Huron
  * Ontario
  * Michigan
  * Erie
  * Superior

---

## 16.6 Action pictures

To learn definitions, students can create vivid action images.

The goal is to connect the sound or meaning of a word to a memorable image.

This makes abstract information easier to recall.

---

## 16.7 Analysis

Analysis improves recall.

Students remember better when they organize, break down, compare, and examine the material themselves.

Useful tools:

* Mind maps
* Graphic organizers
* Visual diagrams
* Categorization
* Multiple-perspective analysis

Important point:

Students recall better when they organize the material themselves instead of only receiving the teacher’s organization.

---

## 16.8 Visual displays

Teachers and students can use:

* Posters
* Diagrams
* Storyboards
* Comic-strip panels
* Overview maps

These should be:

* Easy to read
* Visual
* Illustrated
* Clear
* Left on the wall for weeks when useful

---

## 16.9 Lesson presentation

Recall improves when the most important information appears at the beginning and end of the lesson.

This connects to:

* Primacy effect
* Recency effect

Teachers can:

* Open the lesson with the three most important words.
* Close with the three most important concepts.
* Use music, props, or costumes.
* Start with emotional or controversial discussion.
* End with students sharing what they learned.

---

## 16.10 Teach wholes before parts

Students recall better when they understand the whole context before studying details.

Examples:

* Show a modern video version before studying Shakespeare.
* Give a pictorial overview before analyzing details.
* Teach the whole body before individual anatomy parts.

The brain remembers better with context and complete pictures.

---

## 16.11 Use cliffhangers

Interrupted learning can improve memory.

If the teacher introduces an important problem and leaves students waiting for the solution, curiosity may help memory.

Example:

* Start a mystery or problem at the end of class.
* Let students brainstorm solutions the next day.

---

## 16.12 Peer teaching

Students remember better when they teach others.

They can teach:

* A partner
* A younger student
* A small group
* An adult

Peer teaching works best when students:

* Summarize in their own words
* Break learning into smaller parts
* Debrief the learning
* Discuss what they understood

---

# 17. Rules for Strengthening Learner Memory

Jensen summarizes memory strengthening through four broad principles.

---

## 17.1 More attentional resources = greater content accuracy

To improve attention, teachers can:

* Foster attention
* Ask questions
* Use novelty
* Personalize the learning
* Introduce some manageable stress
* Focus on details

### Explanation

If students pay more attention, the original encoding is more accurate.

---

## 17.2 More neurons = more stable memory

To involve more neurons, teachers can:

* Use movement
* Group and regroup students

### Explanation

Movement and varied grouping increase the amount of brain and body involvement.

---

## 17.3 Greater number of networks = greater accessibility

To create more networks, teachers can:

* Use rhyme
* Use songs
* Use repetition
* Create associations
* Introduce other perspectives

### Explanation

The more pathways connected to learning, the more ways students have to retrieve it.

---

## 17.4 More stable synapses = robust recall

To strengthen synapses, teachers can:

* Allow settling time
* Engage emotions
* Rehearse
* Review
* Use error correction with feedback
* Match earlier states and contexts

### Explanation

Stable synaptic connections make recall stronger and more reliable.

---

# 18. Final Exam-Focused Summary

> [!important] Most testable ideas

## Memory is not a recording

* Memory is not like a video.
* It is a brain change caused by experience.
* Recall is reconstruction.

---

## Teachers influence memory at three stages

1. Encoding
2. Maintenance
3. Retrieval

---

## Memories are malleable

* They can change.
* They can weaken.
* They can become distorted.
* They can be corrected.
* They can be strengthened through review and accurate reactivation.

---

## Humans remember survival-related information best

Strong natural memory systems include:

* Locations
* Procedures
* Emotions
* Conditioned responses

Weak school-related memory often includes:

* Isolated facts
* Names
* Words
* Equations
* Textbook details

---

## Semantic memory is weak

Semantic memory includes facts, words, and textbook information.

It is limited by:

* Time
* Working memory capacity
* Attention
* Lack of meaning
* Lack of intrinsic motivation

Teaching strategies:

* Chunk information
* Pause often
* Use notes
* Pair reteaching
* Summarize
* Compare and contrast
* Use visual representations

---

## Episodic memory is location-based

It depends on:

* Personal experience
* Context
* Location
* Sensory cues

Strength:

* Effortless and powerful.

Weakness:

* Contamination when everything happens in the same place.

Teaching strategies:

* Movement
* Learning stations
* Room changes
* Stand-up reviews
* Outdoor learning

---

## Reflexive memory becomes automatic

It develops through:

* Intense sensory/emotional input
* Repetition

Teaching strategies:

* Flashcards
* Overlearning
* Songs
* Raps
* Repeated cues

---

## Procedural memory is body-based

It includes:

* Skills
* Habits
* Movement
* Hands-on learning

Teaching strategies:

* Role-play
* Projects
* Experiments
* Manipulatives
* Drama
* Games
* Movement

---

## Emotion strengthens memory

Teachers can use:

* Celebration
* Positive rituals
* Storytelling
* Suspense
* Surprise
* Unusual sensory experiences

---

## Memory maintenance requires review

Forgetting happens because of:

1. Transience
2. Absent-mindedness
3. Blocking
4. Misattribution
5. Suggestibility
6. Bias
7. Persistence

Review should include:

* Daily review
* Weekly review
* Error correction
* Feedback
* Mind maps
* Paired sharing

---

## Retrieval depends on cues and conditions

Recall improves when:

* Learning and testing states match.
* Locations match.
* Emotional states match.
* Students receive retrieval cues.
* Students are primed.
* Students get wait time.

---

## Best teaching principle

The strongest learning uses multiple pathways at the same time:

* Words
* Images
* Emotion
* Movement
* Context
* Repetition
* Discussion
* Review
* Feedback

> [!important] One-sentence exam answer
> Effective memory-based teaching does not rely on lecture alone; it strengthens encoding, maintenance, and retrieval by using emotion, movement, context, repetition, review, priming, and multiple memory pathways.
